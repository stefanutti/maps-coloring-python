# Building the Map from the Beach

## A coastal architecture for the Four Color Theorem — version 16

### Remaining proof obligations

| scope | statement still to prove within this program |
|---|---|
| essential | R₅ (§10.3): exclude the remaining balanced bad-coloring case (§10.9) |
| stronger route | escape from every relevant R2 Kempe class, through a separator or a remote sequence (§§9.17–9.18) |
| optional three-move route | a terminating gap-3 reduction, repairs for larger gaps, and compatible sequential choices (§§9.5–9.6, §11) |

The Four Color Theorem is already known. The open statements here concern an independent structural proof program. **This document does not supply a new proof of the theorem.** Results are labelled [CLASSICAL], [PROVED], [PARTIAL], [OPEN], or [CONDITIONAL]. No exhaustive computational verification is used as a proof.

The structural theory dismantles and rebuilds a cubic plane map along a distinguished coastline. The coloring theory describes a single extension through Kempe cycles, transition pairings, and components of two-color face regions. For a fixed gap-2 move, every coloring either admits a short repair or reaches one canonical R2-core.

For any fixed pentagonal face, colorability of the complete map is equivalent to the existence of an alternating coloring window in one of its five edge-deletion intermediates. Their dual colorings belong to one common space. Planar Kempe exchanges relate its boundary sectors and show that a failure of reinsertion would force all five Tait-coloring counts to be equal and divisible by 18, with the three edge-color names fixed. Unequal counts or failure of that divisibility therefore suffice for reinsertion. Excluding the remaining balanced case would complete the single-pentagon argument. The three-face pilot construction is an alternative route with separate larger-gap questions.

## 1. Scope of the program

The Four Color Theorem states that the faces of every plane map can be colored with four colors so that faces sharing an edge receive different colors. The established proofs include the computer-assisted work of Appel and Haken and the later proof of Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas. See the authors' [account of the theorem](https://thomas.math.gatech.edu/FC/fourcolor.html).

The present program uses the following ingredients:

1. Tait's equivalence between four face colors and three edge colors;
2. a reversible coastal construction from the theta graph;
3. exact criteria for extending a coloring by one coastal move;
4. a common dual coloring space for five pentagon deletions, with planar exchange and counting criteria for their existential reinsertion target.

A three-face pilot provides an alternative approach to the final reconstruction.

The remaining issue is the existence of a useful coloring or switch sequence. The boundary word, an incidence path, and an exact test for a specified switch each contain useful information, but none alone establishes that a repair is reachable.

## 2. The target class

### 2.1 Reduction to bridgeless cubic maps

A map is a connected graph embedded on the sphere. Two faces are adjacent only when they share an edge, not when they merely meet at a point.

**It is enough to color bridgeless cubic maps** [CLASSICAL].

- A bridge has the same face on both sides and represents no adjacency between two different regions. Delete all bridges. The resulting nontrivial components are joined by the deleted bridges in a tree, and every edge remaining in such a component lies on a cycle; hence every nontrivial component has minimum degree at least 2. A cyclic component is directly face-colorable. In every other component, suppress maximal degree-2 paths. This produces a bridgeless plane multigraph of minimum degree at least 3, possibly with vertices of degree greater than 3.
- Replace every vertex of degree d ≥ 4 by a small d-gon, attaching the incident edges in their cyclic order. Each new vertex is cubic. Bridgelessness is preserved: every polygon edge lies on the new polygon, and every old cycle through the expanded vertex reroutes along a polygonal subpath. The polygon creates one new face adjacent to the old face-sectors around the vertex. These extra constraints are harmless for a reduction: after a proper coloring of the enlarged map, discard the new face and contract the polygon; every original edge-adjacency remains properly colored.
- Root the tree of bridge-components and restore the components outward from the root. Before attaching a child component, apply a global permutation of its four face colors so that its attachment face has the color already fixed on the parent side. Each child has only one previously fixed interface, so the choices are consistent. Isolated vertices left after deleting bridges impose no face-adjacency constraint.

Hence it suffices to prove that every bridgeless cubic plane map is 4-face-colorable.

### 2.2 Euler's law of twelve

For a cubic map on the sphere,

V − E + F = 2,  3V = 2E,  Σ n·F[n] = 2E.

Eliminating V and E gives

**Σ (6 − n) F[n] = 12.**  (2.1)

A face of size n therefore carries charge 6 − n. Pentagons contribute +1, hexagons 0, and larger faces negative charge.

### 2.3 Minimal counterexamples and the two graph classes

Assume a smallest non-Tait-colorable bridgeless cubic plane map exists. Classical small-face and separator reductions place its dual among internally 6-connected triangulations. In particular the dual is simple and 5-connected, and every primal face has size at least 5. This is the classical minimal-counterexample reduction, used independently of the computer-assisted reducibility part of the known theorem; see Theorem 10 in Thomas's [survey](https://thomas.math.gatech.edu/PAP/update.pdf).

For precision, let **C** be the class of bridgeless cubic plane maps whose dual is a simple 5-connected triangulation. Its members have the following properties:

- every face has size at least 5;
- no cut of at most four edges separates two subgraphs each containing a cycle: the map is *cyclically 5-edge-connected*;
- two faces share at most one edge, and three faces share at most one vertex.

The cut property follows from plane bond–cycle duality: a cyclic bond of length at most four would give a dual cycle of that length with dual vertices on both sides, contradicting 5-connectivity. The incidence properties are proved in §5.5. Internal 6-connectivity is stronger than the conditions used here; it is not identified with cyclic 5-edge-connectivity.

A **current map** is a bridgeless, loopless cubic plane map obtained during excavation or reconstruction. It may have parallel edges and faces of size less than 5. It need not belong to C. Statements about the final map's connectivity are applied to a current map only through a separate transfer argument, such as the cut-lifting lemma in §9.17.

## 3. Tait colorings, Kempe cycles, and the incidence graph

### 3.1 Tait's equivalence

Use the Klein group

V₄ = {0, A, B, C},  A + B = C,  B + C = A,  C + A = B,  g + g = 0.

Given a proper 4-coloring of the faces, color each edge by the sum of the colors of its incident faces. Every edge receives a nonzero value, and the three incident edge colors at every cubic vertex are A, B, C in some order.

Conversely, given such a proper 3-edge-coloring, fix one face color to be 0 and integrate edge colors across the dual graph. The sum around every dual face is zero because the three edge colors around the corresponding primal vertex sum to zero; therefore the face color is path-independent.

**Tait's theorem** [CLASSICAL]. A bridgeless cubic plane map is 4-face-colorable if and only if it has a proper 3-edge-coloring.

A proper 3-edge-coloring will be called a **Tait coloring**.

### 3.2 Kempe cycles

Fix two colors, say {A,B}. Every vertex has exactly one incident A-edge and one incident B-edge, so the {A,B}-subgraph is a disjoint union of even cycles.

- Swapping A and B on one complete cycle preserves the Tait coloring.
- Such a switch does not change the {A,B}-subgraph as a set of edges, hence does not change its cycle partition.
- A switch in a different pair can reroute the {A,B}-cycles.

The last point must be used carefully. A switch may leave the visible boundary word unchanged, even up to reversal and renaming, while changing the global cycle incidences that decide whether a later half-switch is possible.

In a completed Tait coloring every two-color component is a closed Kempe cycle; **there are no open Kempe chains**. Open arcs occur only transiently, when a cycle is cut at the two subdivision points during a half-switch. The obstruction studied in this document is therefore never one of existence — the cycles are always there — but one of **partition**: whether two specified edges lie on the same cycle of the selected pair.

**Global-renaming lemma** [PROVED]. Every permutation of the three edge colors is realizable by a finite sequence of full Kempe switches.

*Proof.* It is enough to realize a transposition, say A↔B. The {A,B}-subgraph is a disjoint union of cycles. Switching every one of those cycles interchanges A and B on every edge of those colors and fixes all C-edges. Since transpositions generate S₃, every global color permutation is obtainable. ∎

Consequently, global renaming never leaves the current edge-Kempe equivalence class. A switch-based argument starts within one such class; no assumption is made that arbitrary Tait colorings are mutually reachable. The terminology agrees with [belcastro and Haas](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.1730).

### 3.3 Edge cuts and color sums

Two elementary facts about cuts will be used repeatedly, both in the endgame analysis and in §9.17.

**Color-sum lemma** [PROVED]. Let φ be a Tait coloring of a cubic graph G and let S be a set of vertices. Then

Σ_{e ∈ ∂S} φ(e) = 0 in V₄.

*Proof.* Add the three incident colors at every vertex of S. Each vertex contributes A+B+C = 0. Every edge with both ends in S is counted twice and cancels, and every edge of ∂S is counted once. ∎

**Monochromatic-cut corollary** [PROVED]. Let S be a nonempty proper vertex subset. Suppose all edges of ∂S have the same color. Then:

1. ∂S is a matching, since two cut edges cannot meet at a vertex;
2. |∂S| is even;
3. both G[S] and G[V∖S] contain cycles, so ∂S is a *cyclic* edge cut;
4. if G is cyclically 5-edge-connected, then |∂S| ≥ 6.

*Proof.* (1) A vertex has only one incident edge of each color. (2) The color-sum lemma gives |∂S|·g = 0 for the common nonzero value g, so |∂S| is even. (3) Every vertex of S carries at most one cut edge, hence |∂S| ≤ |S|. Counting degrees in G[S] gives 2e(S) = 3|S| − |∂S| ≥ 2|S|, so e(S) ≥ |S| and G[S] contains a cycle; the same computation applies to the other side. (4) Cyclic 5-edge-connectivity forces |∂S| ≥ 5, and |∂S| is even. ∎

### 3.4 The Kempe-loop incidence graph

The fact that the three two-color subgraphs consist entirely of closed cycles equips every Tait coloring with a global incidence structure.

For each color X ∈ {A,B,C} let M_X be the perfect matching of X-edges, and let F_AB, F_BC, F_AC be the three 2-factors as above. Define a multigraph **Λ_φ**:

- one node for each Kempe cycle of each of the three 2-factors;
- one incidence edge for each edge e of G: an A-edge joins its {A,B}-cycle to its {A,C}-cycle; a B-edge joins its {A,B}-cycle to its {B,C}-cycle; a C-edge joins its {A,C}-cycle to its {B,C}-cycle.

Every edge of G belongs to exactly two of the three 2-factors, so Λ_φ is well defined and has exactly E(G) incidence edges. Nodes of the same pair are never adjacent — every incidence joins cycles of two *different* pairs — so two distinct cycles of one pair are at distance at least 2 in Λ_φ. The degree of a node equals the length of the corresponding Kempe cycle.

**Vertex-triangle observation** [PROVED]. The three Kempe cycles through one vertex v of G span a triangle in Λ_φ.

*Proof.* The three edges at v have colors A, B, C. At v, each pair of the three cycle-nodes is joined by the incidence contributed by the edge whose color lies in both corresponding color pairs. ∎

**Kempe-loop incidence lemma** [PROVED]. If G is connected, then Λ_φ is connected.

*Proof.* Every vertex of G contributes a connected triangle. If two vertices of G are adjacent through an edge e, their triangles share the entire incidence edge contributed by e. Since G is connected, the union of all vertex triangles is connected. This union is Λ_φ: every incidence edge comes from a primal edge, and every cycle-node occurs in the triangle of each primal vertex on that Kempe cycle. ∎

The same edge-sharing argument gives a stronger fact.

**Kempe-loop 2-connectivity theorem** [PROVED]. If G is connected, then the underlying simple graph of Λ_φ has no cut vertex.

*Proof.* Order the vertices of G along a rooted spanning tree, with each vertex after the first adjacent to an earlier one. The first vertex contributes a triangle, which has no cut vertex. At each later step, the new vertex-triangle shares with the previous union the two endpoints and the incidence edge corresponding to its tree edge. The union of two graphs without cut vertices that share at least two vertices again has no cut vertex: after deleting any one vertex, a surviving common vertex keeps the two parts connected. Induction proves the claim. ∎

**Two-path corollary** [PROVED]. Between any two distinct nodes of Λ_φ there are two internally vertex-disjoint incidence paths.

*Proof.* The underlying simple graph has at least three vertices and no cut vertex, so the vertex form of Menger's theorem applies. ∎

Thus two marked Kempe cycles are never separated by a single cycle-node. A hostile state (§9.18) cannot fail because the marked cycles belong to disconnected loop systems, and any incidence obstruction must involve at least a two-node separator. Section 9.16 identifies the only window-visible two-node separator relevant to a remote-chain argument, and §9.17 translates it into the primal map.

## 4. The coastal construction

### 4.1 States and moves

A **state** is a connected, bridgeless, loopless cubic map on the sphere with one distinguished disk face, the **ocean**. Its boundary cycle is the **coastline**, of length C.

The initial state is the **theta graph**: two vertices joined by three parallel edges, giving two land 2-gons and one ocean 2-gon.

A **move** is performed as follows.

1. Choose interior points p, q on coastal edges e_p, e_q.
2. Choose one coastal stretch from p to q; let k be the number of whole coastal edges strictly inside it.
3. Draw a new arc from p to q through the ocean.
4. The arc and the chosen stretch bound the newborn face.

If e_p ≠ e_q, or if p, q lie on the same edge and the long coastal stretch is selected, the newborn size is

n = k + 3.

In the same-edge long-stretch case, k = C−1, so n = C+2 and the remaining ocean is a 2-gon. If p, q lie on the same edge and the short middle segment is selected, then k = 0 and the newborn face is a 2-gon. The coastline formula below holds in all cases.

Each endpoint subdivides a coastal edge. The land face behind that edge gains one side; this is a **promotion**.

### 4.2 Coastline arithmetic

The move removes two partial coastal pieces and k whole coastal edges from the ocean boundary, and inserts the two outer partial pieces plus the new arc. Therefore

**C′ = C + 4 − n.**  (4.1)

This formula includes the 2-gon move, with n = 2.

Applying (2.1) to an intermediate state, with the ocean counted as a face of size C, gives

Σ_land (6 − n) F[n] − C = 6.  (4.2)

**Running invariant** [PROVED]. Land charge minus coastline length is always 6.

### 4.3 Sealing

A coastal face is **sealed** when a move consumes its last coastal edge.

**Sealing rule.** A move is legal only if every face sealed by it currently has at least 5 sides.

A sealed face never changes again, since all later moves occur on the coastline. The endpoint land faces are not sealed by the move: each retains a nonempty part of the subdivided edge. For distinct split edges this is an outer piece; in the same-edge long-stretch case the common land face retains the middle segment.

**A forward move preserves bridgelessness** [PROVED]. The new arc separates the newborn face from the ocean; each split edge still separates two distinct faces; untouched edges keep their two incident faces.

The final ocean may be declared the closing face only when its boundary has size at least 5.

### 4.4 Debt and budget

A face born with fewer than 5 sides must receive enough promotions before it can be sealed. Such small faces are temporary scaffolding.

Let P be the number of buried pentagons and let

D = Σ_{buried, n≥7} (n − 6).

Every coastal face has charge at most 4, and there are at most C coastal faces. From (4.2),

D − P ≤ 3C − 6.  (4.3)

Long negative-charge wells require a sufficiently long coastline; as the coast shrinks, positive charge must already have been buried.

## 5. Excavation and piloting

### 5.1 Reverse moves

To **excavate** a shore face f, delete its unique coastal edge and suppress the two resulting degree-2 vertices.

In a bridgeless cubic map, the three faces around a vertex are distinct. Consequently, if two faces meet at a vertex, they also share one of the incident edges. Thus a face cannot have an isolated point-contact with the ocean: its contact with the ocean is completely represented by its coastal edges.

A shore face is **clean** when its coastal contact is connected. At a coastal vertex, the two land faces behind the consecutive coastal edges are distinct: otherwise the third incident edge would have the same face on both sides and would be a bridge. Hence consecutive coastal edges have different land owners, and a clean face owns exactly one coastal edge.

The reverse calculus is:

- **E1.** A face is excavatable exactly when it is clean, subject to the loop guard.
- **E2.** Suppressing an endpoint fuses the adjacent coastal edge with the inland edge separating the excavated face from the flanking face; the flanking face loses one side.
- **E3.** The move is forbidden if such a fusion would create a loop. This can happen only when the flanking face is currently a 2-gon.
- **E4.** Excavating a current n-gon changes the coastline by C → C + n − 4.
- **E5.** If the original map has all faces of size at least 5, the reversed construction automatically satisfies the sealing rule. A face first becomes coastal at its final frozen size; only later reverse moves can shorten it.
- **E6.** Legal excavation preserves bridgelessness.

For E6, deleting the unique coastal edge merges the excavated face with the ocean. No untouched edge acquires the merged face on both sides, because that would give a second edge between the excavated face and the ocean. Each suppression replaces two edges separating the same two distinct faces by one edge separating those faces. The loop guard excludes loops.

### 5.2 The Extension Theorem

**Extension Theorem** [PROVED]. If at least three land faces remain, some legal excavation is available.

*Proof.* A bridgeless cubic graph has no cut vertex: if deleting a vertex separated the graph, one component would be attached through only one of the three incident edges, making that edge a bridge. Hence every facial boundary, including the coastline, is a cycle.

First suppose every shore face is clean. Choose any shore face. The loop obstruction discussed below can occur only in the theta graph, which has two land faces, so the chosen face is legally excavatable.

Now suppose some shore face is not clean. Choose two consecutive components of its coastal contact in the cyclic order along the coastline, choose one coastal edge from each component at the ends of the intervening interval, and join interior points of those edges by a simple arc inside the face. Together with the intervening coastline subarc, this bounds a land-side pocket. Its coastline side contains no other coastal edge of the chosen face. Measure the pocket by the number of whole coastal edges on that subarc. The measure is positive because two coastal edges of the same face are never consecutive.

Choose such a pocket of minimum measure. Let g be the face owning a whole coastal edge strictly inside the coastline side of the pocket. Then g is different from the face defining the pocket. Near the chosen edge, the interior of g lies inside the pocket; it cannot leave without crossing either the coastline or the chord. Hence every coastal edge of g lies on the same pocket subarc.

If g were not clean, take two consecutive components of its coastal contact along that subarc. An arc inside g joining them cuts off a smaller positive pocket, contradicting minimality. Thus g is clean.

It remains to exclude the loop obstruction. Let c be the unique coastal edge of a clean face f, and suppose that a flanking face b at one endpoint is a 2-gon. The coastal edge of b and the edge shared by b and f are parallel. At their other endpoint, cubicity forces the third edge to separate f from the ocean, so it is another coastal edge of f. Cleanliness forces this edge to be c. All three edges then join the same two vertices; connectedness makes the whole map the theta graph. By Euler's formula, a cubic map with exactly three faces has two vertices and three edges, so this is precisely the terminal state with two land faces. Since at least three land faces remain, the obstruction is impossible. ∎

### 5.3 Reversed shellings

**Dictionary** [PROVED]. Declaring one face to be the ocean and excavating until only two land faces remain is equivalent, after reversing the order, to a legal coastal construction from the theta graph. The declared ocean becomes the closing face.

The process is a polygonal version of a shelling of the sphere. The direct proof above is included because the coastal moves contain suppressions and a loop guard not present in the usual simplicial formulation.

### 5.4 Piloting three final faces

Let M ∈ C and fix a vertex v with incident faces f₁, f₂, f₃.

**Piloting Lemma** [PROVED]. Suppose there is a face f_ω adjacent to f₁ along an edge not incident to v, and f_ω shares no edge or vertex with f₂ ∪ f₃. Then there is a construction whose final face additions are

f₃, then f₂, then f₁,

followed by declaring f_ω to be the closing face. The vertex v is created during the addition of f₂, the penultimate face-addition.

*Proof.* Declare f_ω to be the ocean. Excavate f₁, then f₂, then f₃.

- By tameness, f₁ meets f_ω in one edge, so f₁ is clean.
- After removing f₁, the only contact of f₂ with the ocean is the edge f₁f₂ at v; the hypothesis excludes any contact with f_ω.
- Excavating f₂ suppresses v and fuses the two edges separating f₃ from f₁ and f₂. Hence f₃ becomes clean.

The loop-guard argument in §5.2 proves that a clean shore face can fail to be excavatable only in the theta graph. The present map and its first two excavations have more than three faces. Thus each of the three specified clean excavations is legal.

Continue with the Extension Theorem until theta, then reverse. ∎

The lemma does not make v the last vertex created: the final addition of f₁ creates two later vertices elsewhere. What the lemma guarantees is that the three faces around v form the last three face additions in a prescribed order.

Note also that the hypothesis on f_ω keeps f₂ and f₃ away from the coastal edge of f₁: the two faces demoted by the excavation of f₁ are neither f₂ nor f₃. This is used in §9.1.

### 5.5 The dual lemma

Let G = M* be the dual triangulation of a map M ∈ C. Let a, b, c correspond to f₁, f₂, f₃; the primal vertex v corresponds to the facial triangle abc.

The 5-connectivity in the definition of C is sufficient for the following lemma.

**Dual Lemma** [PROVED].

1. Two primal faces share at most one edge, and three primal faces share at most one vertex.
2. For any choice of f₁ among the three faces at v, a suitable closing face f_ω exists.

*Proof.* Simplicity of G excludes parallel dual edges, proving the first two-face statement. If three primal faces met at two vertices, the dual triangle on their vertices would either bound two faces or be a separating nonfacial triangle; both contradict 5-connectivity.

The neighbors of a form a cycle of length at least 5. A chord between nonconsecutive link vertices would make a nonfacial triangle through a; in a plane triangulation that triangle is separating, contrary to 5-connectivity. Hence the link cycle is induced. The vertices b, c are consecutive on it. Choose a neighbor d of a that is neither b, c nor either of their outer neighbors on the link. Inducedness gives db, dc ∉ E(G). The primal face dual to d is adjacent to f₁ along an edge away from v and cannot share a primal vertex with f₂ or f₃, since such a shared vertex would be a dual triangular face containing d and b or c. ∎

The same link argument gives a quantitative form of the choice.

**Pilot-multiplicity corollary** [PROVED]. Fix a corner with incident face sizes n₁, n₂, n₃. If one incident face of size n_i is assigned to f₁, then there are at least n_i − 4 choices for the closing face and two orders for the other two faces. Hence the corner has at least

2[(n₁−4)+(n₂−4)+(n₃−4)] = 2(n₁+n₂+n₃−12)

ordered pilots. In particular, every corner whose incident faces all have size at least 5 has at least six.

*Proof.* In the induced link cycle of the dual vertex corresponding to the chosen f₁, exclude the two neighbors corresponding to the other corner faces and their two outer link-neighbors. The remaining n_i − 4 link vertices all satisfy the Dual Lemma. The Piloting Lemma is symmetric in the order of the other two faces. Summing over the three choices of f₁ gives the formula. ∎

## 6. Positive curvature at vertices

Give a vertex incident with faces of sizes n₁, n₂, n₃ the charge

c(v) = 6/n₁ + 6/n₂ + 6/n₃ − 3.

Each n-gon contributes 6/n at each of its n corners, so

Σ_v c(v) = 6F − 3V = 12.  (6.1)

Assume 5 ≤ n₁ ≤ n₂ ≤ n₃. Positive charge means

1/n₁ + 1/n₂ + 1/n₃ > 1/2.

If n₁ ≥ 6, the sum is at most 1/2. Thus n₁ = 5. If n₂ ≥ 7, the sum is at most 1/5 + 2/7 < 1/2. For n₂ = 5 the inequality gives n₃ < 10; for n₂ = 6 it gives n₃ < 15/2. The only possibilities are therefore

(5,5,5), (5,5,6), (5,5,7), (5,5,8), (5,5,9), (5,6,6), (5,6,7).

Their charges are respectively

3/5, 2/5, 9/35, 3/20, 1/15, 1/5, 2/35.

**Positive-corner theorem** [PROVED]. Every cubic plane map with all faces of size at least 5 contains one of these seven corner types.

Since every positive vertex has charge at most 3/5 and the total charge is 12, there are **at least 20 positive vertices** [PROVED]. The weighted count in §9.19 gives **at least 120 pilots even after requiring a pentagon first and the smaller of the other two faces second**. Each has a legal excavation continuation.

For M ∈ C, the union of the three incident faces is a disk: their pairwise shared edges form a Y at the common vertex, and tameness excludes any further contact. Its boundary has length

n₁ + n₂ + n₃ − 6,

between 9 and 13.

## 7. What the induction has gained

For the three-face route, a pilot determines the intermediate map M⁻ obtained by excavating f₁, f₂, f₃. The Extension Theorem then completes the excavation to theta. That continuation certifies a coastal construction, but it does not change M⁻ or the three final moves. The genuine coloring choices are the pilot, the coloring of its intermediate map, and the compatible sequence of switches and half-switches.

The single-pentagon route of §10 requires only one excavation. It has five choices of deleted edge for any chosen pentagon and does not require positive-corner piloting. Both routes rely on minimality to color a smaller current map. Neither route makes the Kempe cycles local: a cycle through a boundary edge may travel arbitrarily far from the window.

## 8. A single coloring extension

### 8.1 The working pair

A move subdivides two coastal edges e_p, e_q and inserts a new arc t. Let the old colors be x = φ(e_p) and y = φ(e_q). If the new arc has color τ, then at each new cubic vertex the two halves of the split edge must receive the two colors different from τ.

Thus τ determines a **working pair**: the two colors not equal to τ. A pair P is **admissible** for the move exactly when x, y ∈ P.

### 8.2 Half-switch criterion

If the two split edges lie on the same Kempe cycle of the working pair, cut that cycle at the subdivision points and switch one of the two resulting arcs. The split halves then show the two working colors at each new vertex, and the new arc receives the third color.

Call this **CONDITION-1**.

If the two split edges lie on distinct cycles of the selected pair, call it **CONDITION-2**.

- If x ≠ y, the working pair is forced to be {x,y}.
- If x = y, there are two candidate working pairs, one for each choice of the second color.
- A 2-gon move always extends directly: if the subdivided edge has color x, keep x on the two outer pieces, give the middle piece a second color y, and give the new arc the third color; each new vertex then sees all three colors.

**Half-switch lemma** [PROVED]. A half-switch extension exists for a selected working pair exactly when CONDITION-1 holds.

### 8.3 Difference-field formulation

Lift the old coloring to the subdivided old edges: both halves inherit the parent-edge color. For an extension ψ, define on those old-edge pieces

δ(e) = φ(e) + ψ(e).

At every old vertex, the incident differences sum to zero. At each new vertex, the two split-edge differences sum to the new arc color τ. Equivalently, if the new arc is assigned difference τ, the complete difference field has zero sum at every vertex.

If δ takes only one nonzero value on its old-edge support, that value must be τ; its support is a path between the two subdivision points in the working-pair subgraph, possibly together with disjoint complete cycles.

**Constant-support classification** [PROVED]. The one-value extensions are exactly the half-switches, up to independent full switches in the same pair.

*Proof.* Suppose the nonzero value of δ is g. At every old cubic vertex, the zero-sum condition forces the old-edge support of δ to have degree 0 or 2. At each subdivision point exactly one old-edge piece is in the noncyclic support, and the new arc contributes τ; hence g = τ. Moreover an old edge of color τ cannot lie in the support, because adding τ would turn its color into 0. The support therefore lies in the 2-factor formed by the two colors different from τ. Its noncyclic part is a path between the subdivision points, and in a 2-factor that path is one of the two arcs obtained by cutting a single Kempe cycle. Every remaining component is a complete cycle. Switching the path gives the half-switch, and switching the extra cycles gives the independent full switches. The converse is immediate. ∎

This does not prove that every possible extension is reachable by full Kempe switches followed by a half-switch. Accordingly:

> A move is **repairable** from a given coloring when some finite sequence of full Kempe switches reaches a coloring satisfying CONDITION-1 for an admissible working pair.

## 9. The piloted endgame

### 9.1 Geometry of the last three additions

Let the final face additions be T₃, T₂, T₁, building f₃, f₂, f₁. Let the final face sizes be n₃, n₂, n₁.

Careful reversal of the three excavations gives:

- T₃ has gap k₃ = n₃ − 4 and creates f₃ with size n₃ − 1;
- T₂ has gap k₂ = n₂ − 3, creates v, and promotes f₃ to size n₃;
- T₁ has gap k₁ = n₁ − 3 and seals f₂, f₃ at their final sizes.

The two faces demoted by the excavation of f₁ are the flanking faces of the edge f₁f_ω; by the remark at the end of §5.4 they are neither f₂ nor f₃, so f₂ and f₁ are both born at their final size, while f₃ alone is promoted once.

If the closing face has size n_ω, the coastline before T₃ is

C⁻ = n_ω + n₁ + n₂ + n₃ − 13.

The intermediate map M⁻ is the map obtained after excavating f₁, f₂, f₃; it is not literally an induced submap, because the excavations also suppress vertices and fuse edges.

### 9.2 The pentagon opening

Every positive corner contains a pentagon. Assign a pentagon to f₃. Then k₃ = 1, so the boundary window is

E_a, m, E_b.

**Pentagon Opening Theorem** [PROVED]. The move T₃ always extends after at most one full Kempe switch.

*Proof.* Let the colors be x, z, y in the displayed order. The middle color differs from both endpoint colors.

If x = y, choose the pair {x,z}. The three boundary edges form a path in one {x,z}-cycle, so CONDITION-1 holds.

If x ≠ y, the middle edge has the third color. The forced pair is {x,y}. If the endpoints already share a cycle, we are done. Otherwise switch the {x,y}-cycle through E_a. It cannot contain E_b and cannot contain the middle edge. The endpoint colors become equal, reducing to the previous case. ∎

Thus only T₂ and T₁ remain.

### 9.3 Runs, cleaning and shifting

The local gap analyses use a common calculus. In this section the window is a simple coastal path with distinct split edges, as in the endgames considered below. Let the window of a move be the sequence of consecutive coastal edges

w = (e₀, e₁, …, e_{k+1}),

with split edges e₀ and e_{k+1} and k gap edges between them. Fix an admissible pair P = {U,V}, and let W denote the third color.

Since consecutive window edges have different colors, no two W-colored window edges are adjacent. Deleting the W-letters therefore leaves nonempty blocks.

> The **P-runs** of the window are the maximal blocks of consecutive window edges whose colors lie in P. Write them R₁, …, R_s, separated by single W-letters. Thus s = 1 + (number of W-letters), and the first and last runs contain the split edges.

**Run lemma** [PROVED]. All edges of one run lie on a single P-cycle. Consequently CONDITION-1 holds exactly when R₁ and R_s lie on the same P-cycle; in particular s = 1 implies CONDITION-1.

*Proof.* Two consecutive window edges with colors in P share a vertex and are therefore consecutive edges of the P-cycle through them. ∎

Two elementary switches act on a single W-letter. Their hypotheses specify the entire intersection of the switched cycle with the window.

**Cleaning lemma** [PROVED]. Let e_j be a W-letter whose two window neighbours have the *same* color u ∈ P, and let u′ be the other color of P. Let Z be the {u′,W}-cycle through e_j. If Z contains no window edge other than e_j, then switching Z recolors e_j to u′ and leaves every other window edge unchanged. The word remains proper, the two runs adjacent to e_j merge, and s drops by one.

*Proof.* Only edges of colors u′ and W lie on Z. Among the window edges these are e_j and, by hypothesis, no other. After the switch e_j has color u′ ≠ u, so the word stays proper, and the three consecutive edges u, u′, u all have colors in P. ∎

**Shifting lemma** [PROVED]. Let e_j be a W-letter and let e_{j′} be one of its window neighbours, of color u. The {u,W}-cycle Z through e_j automatically contains e_{j′}. If Z contains no further window edge, switching Z exchanges the colors of e_j and e_{j′}: the W-letter moves one position, and every other window edge is unchanged.

*Proof.* The edges e_j and e_{j′} share a vertex and carry the two colors of the pair {u,W}, so they are consecutive on one {u,W}-cycle. Switching it recolors exactly these two window edges. ∎

If the neighbour moved onto by a shift is a split edge, the shift changes the split-edge colors and therefore the set of admissible pairs; this is how words with "hard" letters are converted into words with "easy" ones.

**Hardness dichotomy** [PROVED]. Call a W-letter *easy* when its two window neighbours have equal colors, and *hard* otherwise. A single switch that recolors a hard W-letter and nothing else in the window cannot exist.

*Proof.* Changing the color of e_j requires switching a cycle of a pair containing W, which gives e_j either U or V. Both clash with the neighbour carrying that color, unless that neighbour also changes; by the shifting lemma this is exactly what the corresponding switch does, so the letter moves instead of disappearing. ∎

**Local blocking criterion** [PROVED]. Fix an admissible pair and a window containing a foreign letter. A specified cleaning fails its stated hypothesis exactly when its cleaning cycle contains another window edge. A specified shift fails its stated hypothesis exactly when its shifting cycle contains a window edge beyond the two edges being exchanged. Thus *all* moves supplied by these lemmas are blocked exactly when every candidate has its corresponding extra incidence.

*Proof.* These statements are the negations of the intersection hypotheses in the cleaning and shifting lemmas. A shift already contains two adjacent window edges; the additional obstruction is a third edge, not the required neighbour. ∎

The criterion concerns these local moves only. It neither excludes other successful switches nor proves termination of a sequence of local moves. A word alone does not determine the required cycle-incidence tests.

**Endpoint-cleaning lemma** [PROVED]. Fix a reference pair P = {U,V}. Suppose a window endpoint has the third color W and its unique window neighbour has color u ∈ P. Let u′ be the other color of P. If the {W,u′}-cycle through the endpoint contains no other window edge, switching it recolors that endpoint to u′ and preserves a proper window.

*Proof.* The neighbour has neither color of the switched pair, so it is fixed. All other window edges are fixed by hypothesis. ∎

**Abstract window-normalization theorem** [PROVED]. Fix an initially admissible reference pair P and suppose the window contains m letters of its third color W. There is a prescribed sequence of at most m(k+1) sparse cleaning, shifting and endpoint-cleaning operations that makes the word alternating in P. Each prescribed operation lifts to a full Kempe switch exactly when the specified cycle avoids every other window edge beyond its designated singleton or adjacent pair.

*Proof.* Choose the leftmost W, at position j. The prefix before it alternates in P. If the letter is easy, clean it. If it is hard, shift it one step to the left using the color of its left neighbour. The preceding prefix letter, if present, has the other P-color; consequently the shifted W is still hard. Continue until W reaches e₀, then apply endpoint cleaning. In either case this phase removes one W and leaves every later W fixed.

At every cleaning or endpoint-cleaning step the designated window component of the switched pair is a singleton. At each hard shift it is exactly the designated two-edge path. Thus no other window recoloring is forced by the word itself. Failure to lift a step means that its complete Kempe cycle returns to a different window component of the same pair.

The lexicographic quantity (number of W-letters, position of the leftmost W) strictly decreases at each prescribed operation, taking the second coordinate to be zero when no W remains. A phase uses at most j+1 ≤ k+1 operations. During a leftward sweep P is a reference pair, not necessarily an admissible extension pair; it becomes admissible again when the endpoint is cleaned. After m phases every window edge lies in P and consecutive colors alternate. ∎

**Conditional lifting corollary** [PROVED]. If the cycles selected by this procedure have no extra window incidences at every stage, the corresponding Kempe sequence repairs the move. Therefore a failure of this specified procedure must exhibit a global return of a selected cycle. The potential proves termination of the abstract word operations, not availability of their graph realizations.

### 9.4 Gap 2 and the R2-core

**Status [PARTIAL].** The normal form is complete and the local branching is exhaustive; the hostile-class obstruction remains open.

For a gap of two whole edges, the window has length four. Up to color permutation and reversal, the possible words are

ABAB, ABAC, ABCA.

*Canonical-word convention.* A boundary word is put in canonical form using a reversal of the displayed window and a global permutation of A, B, C. Reversal changes only the notation. By the global-renaming lemma, the color permutation is itself a Kempe sequence, so canonicalization does not invalidate any statement about a Kempe equivalence class.

Write the window as e₀, e₁, e₂, e₃.

- **ABAB.** The forced pair {A,B} has s = 1, so CONDITION-1 holds [PROVED].
- **ABAC.** The forced pair is {A,C} and W = B. There is a single W-letter, e₁, whose neighbours e₀ and e₂ are both A: it is easy. The cleaning cycle is the {B,C}-cycle Z through e₁, and the only other window edge of colors B or C is e₃. Hence either Z avoids e₃ and one switch produces ACAC with s = 1, or Z contains e₃.
- **ABCA.** Both candidate pairs have one hard W-letter: for {A,C} the letter e₁ is flanked by A and C, and for {A,B} the letter e₂ is flanked by B and A. Shift e₂ towards e₃ using the {A,C}-cycle Y through e₂, which automatically contains e₃. The only other window edge of colors A or C is e₀. If Y contains e₀, then e₀ and e₃ lie on one {A,C}-cycle and CONDITION-1 already holds. Otherwise the shift produces the word ABAC, and since an {A,C}-switch does not change the {A,C} 2-factor, the two split edges still lie on distinct {A,C}-cycles.

This proves the following.

**Gap-2 normal-form theorem** [PROVED]. Every gap-2 coloring either is repairable using at most two full Kempe switches before a half-switch, or can be placed, using at most one full switch and a global renaming, in the following form:

- word ABAC;
- e₀ and e₃ lie on distinct {A,C}-cycles;
- e₁ and e₃ lie on the same {B,C}-cycle K.

Call this an **R2-core** state. The alternatives need not be disjoint: a core may admit another repair.

*Proof.* ABAB already satisfies CONDITION-1. In ABAC, either CONDITION-1 holds, or the cleaning switch repairs the move, or its failure gives exactly the displayed core. For ABCA, CONDITION-1 either holds or the shift described above produces ABAC in one switch; its cleaning may require a second switch. ∎

The obvious switch on an R2-core has a rigid behavior.

**R2 toggle theorem** [PROVED]. Let K be the marked {B,C}-cycle of an R2-core. Switch B and C on K.

- The boundary word becomes ACAB.
- If the new endpoint edges lie on one {A,B}-cycle, the move is repairable.
- Otherwise, after globally exchanging the names B and C, the new coloring is again an R2-core.
- In the latter case, the canonical operation "switch K, then exchange the global names B,C" is an involution.

*Proof.* Since K contains e₁ and e₃ but neither A-edge, the word changes from ABAC to ACAB. The new forced working pair is {A,B}. If its endpoint edges share a cycle, CONDITION-1 holds. Otherwise exchange the names B,C; the word returns to ABAC, the forced pair becomes {A,C}, and its endpoints remain on distinct cycles. The {B,C} 2-factor is unchanged as a set by the switch, so the marked cycle still contains the internal and final marked edges. This is again an R2-core. The switch on K commutes with the global transposition of the names B,C, since K is invariant as an edge set; both operations have order two, so their composition also has order two. ∎

This eliminates a misleading possibility: repeatedly applying the marked switch cannot gradually improve a failed R2-core. If the first toggle does not solve the move, it stays in an involutive orbit of size one or two; no iteration can create a descent.

The canonical renaming has a useful switch-theoretic interpretation.

**Complementary-cycle form of the R2 toggle** [PROVED]. Let K be the marked {B,C}-cycle of an R2-core. The canonical operation "switch K, then globally exchange B,C" is exactly the product of the switches on all {B,C}-cycles other than K.

*Proof.* A global exchange of B,C is obtained by switching every component of the {B,C} 2-factor. All these switches commute because their cycles are disjoint. Composing with the additional switch on K cancels the switch on K and leaves precisely the product over the other components. ∎

All {B,C}-cycles other than K are remote from the window. This observation will combine with the remote trichotomy in §9.18.

There are only four switches that can visibly touch an R2-core window.

**Local-switch exhaustion theorem** [PROVED]. In an R2-core, let

- X be the {A,C}-cycle through e₀;
- Y be the distinct {A,C}-cycle through e₂, e₃;
- H be the {A,B}-cycle through e₀, e₁, e₂;
- K be the marked {B,C}-cycle through e₁, e₃.

Their intersections with the window are exactly

X∩W = {e₀},  Y∩W = {e₂,e₃},  H∩W = {e₀,e₁,e₂},  K∩W = {e₁,e₃}.

Every other Kempe-cycle switch is disjoint from all four window edges and therefore leaves the boundary word ABAC unchanged.

*Proof.* In the {A,C} 2-factor, the consecutive edges e₂, e₃ lie on one cycle Y, while the core hypothesis places e₀ on a distinct cycle X; the edge e₁ has color B and lies on neither. In the {A,B} 2-factor, the consecutive pairs e₀,e₁ and e₁,e₂ force all three edges onto one cycle H, while e₃ has color C. In the {B,C} 2-factor, the core hypothesis puts e₁, e₃ on K, while the two A-edges lie on no {B,C}-cycle. A switch on any other component of any of the three 2-factors meets no window edge. ∎

The four visible switches admit exact one-step tests.

**R2 local one-switch table** [PROVED]. Starting from the word ABAC:

| switched cycle | new word | the new coloring satisfies CONDITION-1 exactly when |
|---|---|---|
| X | CBAC | the endpoint edges lie on one new {B,C}-cycle |
| Y | ABCA | the endpoint edges lie on one new {A,B}-cycle |
| H | BABC | the endpoint edges lie on one new {B,C}-cycle |
| K | ACAB | the endpoint edges lie on one new {A,B}-cycle |

In the first two rows the unchanged {A,C} 2-factor still separates the endpoints, so the displayed foreign pair is the only possible successful pair. The last two rows have distinct endpoint colors, so their working pair is forced.

*Proof.* The new words follow directly from the four window intersections above. Switching X or Y leaves the {A,C} 2-factor unchanged, and its two endpoint edges remain on the distinct cycles X and Y; hence only the other candidate pair can work. For H and K, the endpoint colors become distinct, so the half-switch criterion leaves exactly the displayed forced pair. ∎

Thus the local branching is finite and explicit, but it is not automatically successful. A switch disjoint from the window can leave the word unchanged while changing the foreign transition pairings; a visible switch can also be useful only as the first step of a longer sequence. The gap-2 obstruction is therefore global even though its visible window has only four edges.

### 9.5 Gap 3: exact local transitions

**Status [PARTIAL].** The following rules cover every boundary word, but they are not a terminating reduction algorithm. Two explicit blocked patterns are identified; their exhaustiveness as normal forms is not proved.

For a gap of three whole edges, write the window as e₀,e₁,e₂,e₃,e₄. Up to color permutation and reversal, the six words are

ABABA, ABABC, ABACA, ABACB, ABCAB, ABCBA.

Their number follows from the structural word count in §9.6. The representatives are distinct under renaming and reversal and cover the possible equality patterns. In ABABA the admissible pair {A,B} immediately satisfies CONDITION-1. For the other words assume that no admissible pair satisfies CONDITION-1, and use the following rules.

**Gap-3 transition proposition** [PROVED].

1. **ABCAB.** The {A,B}-cycle through e₀,e₁ avoids e₃,e₄. Switching it gives BACAB, which becomes ABCBA after renaming.
2. **ABACA.** The {A,B}-cycle through e₄ avoids e₀,e₁,e₂, since otherwise the equal-colored endpoints would share an admissible cycle. It also avoids the C-edge e₃. Switching it gives ABACB.
3. **ABCBA.** Let Z be the {A,C}-cycle through e₂. If it contains neither endpoint, its switch gives ABABA and repairs the move. It cannot contain both endpoints. If it contains exactly one, its switch gives ABABC after reversal if needed.
4. **ABABC.** Let X₀,X₂,X₄ be the {A,C}-cycles through the indicated edges. Here X₀ ≠ X₄. If X₂ ≠ X₄, switching X₄ changes only e₄ in the window and gives ABABA, repairing the move. The remaining case is X₂ = X₄. Let K be the {B,C}-cycle through the consecutive edges e₃,e₄. If the {B,C}-cycle through e₁ differs from K, switching it gives ACABC, or ABACB after renaming. If it equals K, the state has pattern R3a below.
5. **ABACB.** Let Y be the {A,C}-cycle through e₂,e₃ and K the {B,C}-cycle through e₃,e₄. If Y avoids e₀, switching Y gives ABCAB. If K avoids e₁, switching K gives ABABC. These two tests may both hold. If neither holds, the state has pattern R3b below.

*Proof.* Consecutive edges in a pair lie on its unique cycle through their common vertex. Failure of CONDITION-1 separates the two endpoint cycles for each admissible pair. These facts give every claimed avoidance. Switching the specified window edges then gives the displayed words. ∎

An **R3a pattern** is an ABABC state with X₂ = X₄ ≠ X₀ and with e₁ on the {B,C}-cycle through e₃,e₄. An **R3b pattern** is an ABACB state in which the endpoints lie on distinct {A,B}-cycles, e₀ lies on the {A,C}-cycle through e₂,e₃, and e₁ lies on the {B,C}-cycle through e₃,e₄. The names *R3a-core* and *R3b-core* refer only to these specified patterns, not to a proved exhaustive irreducible list.

**Gap-3 toggle proposition** [PROVED]. In R3a, switching its marked {B,C}-cycle and then globally exchanging B,C restores the word ABABC. In R3b, either switching the marked {A,C}-cycle and exchanging A,C, or switching the marked {B,C}-cycle and exchanging B,C, restores ABACB. Each canonical operation is an involution on colorings with that marked cycle.

*Proof.* The intermediate words are respectively ACACB, CBCAB and ACABC. The switched pair's cycle edge set is unchanged by both operations; switching and globally transposing commute and each has order two. The foreign cycle equalities defining a pattern must be checked again after the operation. ∎

**Gap-3 hostility consequence** [PROVED]. Every hostile gap-3 Kempe class contains a coloring with canonical word ABABC or ABACB. Every ABABC coloring in such a class satisfies X₂ = X₄ ≠ X₀.

*Proof.* The first three transition rules lead to one of these two words unless the move is repaired. In ABABC, the alternative X₂ ≠ X₄ gives a repair by rule 4. ∎

**Refined hostile-family reduction** [PROVED]. Within at most four displayed full switches, up to renaming and reversal, a hostile gap-3 coloring reaches either an R3b pattern or an ABABC coloring with X₂ = X₄ ≠ X₀.

*Proof.* From a non-R3b ABACB coloring, either K avoids e₁ and one switch gives ABABC, or Y avoids e₀ and the route ABACB → ABCAB → ABCBA → ABABC applies. Hostility excludes each immediate-repair exit. An ABACA state first reaches ABACB; the other words need fewer steps. The preceding hostility consequence gives X₂ = X₄ ≠ X₀. ∎

The ABABC family here includes both R3a and the unresolved case in which e₁ is on a different {B,C}-cycle from e₃,e₄. The rules permit a return from ABACB through ABCAB and ABCBA to ABABC, followed by a return to ABACB. Consequently their reversibility leaves a genuine missing step: show that some prescribed sequence repairs the move or reaches a specified core. Excluding R3a and R3b alone would not yet close that step.

### 9.6 Larger gaps

**Status [PARTIAL].** For gaps 4 to 6 no complete classification is given. The cleaning–shifting calculus supplies exact tests for particular local switches.

A gap-k word has length k+2, is written over a three-color alphabet, and has no equal consecutive letters. Count such words up to color permutation and reversal.

Let L = k+2. Burnside's lemma gives:

- the identity fixes all 3·2^(L−1) = 3·2^(k+1) proper words;
- pure reversal fixes no word when L is even, because the two middle letters would coincide; when L = 2h+1, it fixes 3·2^h = 3·2^((k+1)/2) palindromes;
- for a fixed color transposition composed with reversal, an odd-length fixed word has the transposition's fixed color in the middle and 2^h choices on one side; an even-length fixed word has one of the two moved colors at the middle-left position and 2^(h−1) further choices. Summed over the three transpositions, the contribution is 3·2^((k+1)/2) for odd k and 3·2^(k/2+1) for even k;
- a nonidentity color permutation without reversal fixes no proper word, and a 3-cycle composed with reversal fixes none because squaring it would fix every letter by a nontrivial 3-cycle.

Dividing by |S₃ × Z₂| = 12 gives, in the odd and even cases respectively,

2^(k−1) + 2^((k−1)/2),  2^(k−1) + 2^(k/2−1).

Therefore

**Word-count theorem** [PROVED].

W(k) = 2^(k−1) + 2^⌊(k−1)/2⌋.

For gaps 1 through 6, the counts are

2, 3, 6, 10, 20, 36.

The alternating word is always immediately repairable, so gaps 4–6 have 63 non-alternating word types in total. This counts equality patterns of words, not chromatic obstructions or Kempe classes. The blocking criterion in §9.3 specifies what must be checked to apply a chosen cleaning or shift; it does not prove that iterating those operations reaches an alternating word.

**Easy two-run lemma** [PROVED]. Suppose an admissible pair has exactly two runs and its unique foreign letter is easy. Either one cleaning switch repairs the move, or its cleaning cycle contains another window edge in a run containing a split edge.

*Proof.* Successful cleaning joins the two runs into one. If its intersection hypothesis fails, every additional window edge is in one of the two runs, each of which contains a split edge. ∎

This particular blocking pattern will be called the **easy two-run R2 signature**. The assertion is restricted to an easy foreign letter. A hard letter is governed by shifting, and requires separate tests. Transferring a gap-2 repair theorem to larger gaps would require checking every hypothesis of that theorem, including its use of the short window.

### 9.7 Exact surgery law for a foreign switch

For each color X ∈ {A,B,C}, let M_X be the set of edges of color X. In a Tait coloring each M_X is a perfect matching. Write

F_XY = M_X ∪ M_Y.

Thus F_XY is the even 2-factor whose components are the {X,Y} Kempe cycles.

Let K be one component of F_AB, and switch A and B on K. If primes denote the new coloring, then

M_A′ = (M_A \ E(K)) ∪ (M_B ∩ E(K)),
M_B′ = (M_B \ E(K)) ∪ (M_A ∩ E(K)),
M_C′ = M_C.

Consequently,

F_AB′ = F_AB,
F_AC′ = F_AC △ E(K),
F_BC′ = F_BC △ E(K),  (9.1)

where △ denotes symmetric difference.

**Foreign-switch surgery theorem** [PROVED]. Switching one {A,B} Kempe cycle leaves the {A,B} 2-factor unchanged and transforms each foreign 2-factor by symmetric difference with the switched cycle.

*Proof.* The first identity is immediate because the set of A- and B-edges is unchanged. On K, the A-edges are removed from F_AC and the complementary B-edges are inserted; away from K, F_AC is unchanged. This is exactly F_AC △ E(K). The proof for F_BC is symmetric. ∎

This gives an exact transition law. It also explains why a foreign switch can merge or split Kempe cycles: symmetric difference performs surgery on a 2-factor rather than merely relabeling one of its components.

### 9.8 The transition pairing

The surgery in (9.1) can be described combinatorially.

Continue with a switched {A,B} cycle K and the affected factor F_AC. Along K, the edges of F_AC are exactly the A-edges of K; call this alternating perfect matching P_A. Let P_B be the complementary perfect matching of K, formed by its B-edges.

Delete P_A from F_AC. Every vertex of K then has degree 1, while every other vertex in the remaining graph has degree 2. Hence every component meeting K is a path with two endpoints on K. These paths pair the vertices of K; denote the resulting perfect matching by

Q_AC(K).

After contracting each such path to one abstract edge, the old and new cycle systems are obtained by two different closures:

old {A,C}-cycles meeting K: Q_AC(K) ∪ P_A,
new {A,C}-cycles meeting K: Q_AC(K) ∪ P_B.  (9.2)

The same construction, with the roles of A and B exchanged, describes the change in F_BC.

**Transition-pairing theorem** [PROVED]. The complete effect of a foreign switch on an affected Kempe-cycle partition is determined by the pairing Q(K) induced by the paths of the old 2-factor outside the alternating matching on the switched cycle.

*Proof.* A component of a graph of maximum degree 2 is a path or a cycle. After deleting P_A, every component that meets K has degree-1 vertices precisely at its contacts with K, and therefore is a path with exactly two such contacts. Restoring P_A joins these paths according to the old alternating matching; restoring P_B joins the same paths according to the complementary matching. This gives (9.2). ∎

Planarity adds a useful restriction. The cycle K is a Jordan curve. The interior of each path defining Q_AC(K) is disjoint from K, so the path lies entirely on one side of K. Paths on the same side are disjoint and therefore induce a noncrossing matching of their endpoints in the cyclic order on K.

**Two-page corollary** [PROVED]. The transition pairing is the union of two noncrossing partial matchings, one drawn on each side of the switched Kempe cycle.

### 9.9 Binary domain-wall calculus

The Klein-group formulation has a useful two-bit interpretation. Write

A = (1,0), B = (0,1), C = (1,1),

and write every face color as (p,q) ∈ Z₂². Put r = p+q. Then

D_p = F_AC,  D_q = F_BC,  D_r = F_AB,  D_r = D_p △ D_q.  (9.3)

Indeed, an edge belongs to D_p exactly when its color has first bit 1, hence when its color is A or C; the other identities are identical.

Each D_p, D_q, D_r is therefore a family of disjoint Jordan cycles: it is the domain wall of one binary face field.

A switch on one component K of D_p = F_AC adds B = (0,1) to all face colors on one side of K. Thus it flips q on that side, leaves p fixed, and gives

D_p′ = D_p,  D_q′ = D_q △ K,  D_r′ = D_r △ K.

The analogous statements hold cyclically for switches on D_q and D_r.

**Domain-wall theorem** [PROVED]. Up to a global additive normalization of the face colors, a Tait coloring is equivalently a pair of binary face fields (p,q) whose three domain-wall systems D_p, D_q, D_{p+q} are 2-factors. A Kempe switch fixes one domain-wall component and toggles that Jordan curve into each of the other two systems.

This is the face-color version of the foreign-switch surgery theorem. It shows that the open problem is a problem about surgery among three coupled planar loop systems, not merely about words in three letters.

### 9.10 Region partitions and disk flips

The domain-wall formulation can be sharpened by keeping the regions on the two sides of each two-color system.

Normalize the integrated face coloring by assigning color 0 to the ocean. For an unordered working pair P = {U,V}, let W be the third nonzero color and define

X_P = f⁻¹({U,V}),  X_Pᶜ = f⁻¹({0,W}).  (9.4)

An edge separates X_P from X_Pᶜ exactly when its Tait color lies in {U,V}. Hence

∂X_P = F_UV.  (9.5)

Thus the components of the boundary of X_P are precisely the {U,V} Kempe cycles. Changing the additive normalization of the face coloring may exchange X_P and X_Pᶜ, but it does not change the underlying partition.

**Region dictionary** [PROVED]. Every two-color Kempe system is the boundary between the two parts of a binary partition of the faces.

Now let Δ be a Kempe cycle of pair {R,S}, and let G = R+S be the remaining nonzero element of V₄. Let D be either disk bounded by the Jordan curve Δ.

**Disk-flip theorem** [PROVED]. Switching R and S on Δ is obtained at face level by

f′ = f + G·1_D.  (9.6)

The partition whose boundary is the {R,S} system is unchanged. Each of the other two face partitions is toggled inside D.

*Proof.* An edge outside Δ has both incident faces on the same side of Δ, so the two added values cancel in its edge difference. An edge of Δ has exactly one incident face in D, so its color is increased by G; this interchanges R and S. Addition by G preserves the two face classes defining the {R,S} partition and exchanges the two classes of each foreign partition. ∎

In particular, for a fixed working pair P, a switch on a P-cycle leaves X_P unchanged, while a switch on either foreign pair sends

X_P ↦ X_P △ D.  (9.7)

This is stronger than the edge identity ∂(X_P △ D) = ∂X_P △ Δ: it retains the component structure of the regions.

### 9.11 The contact tree

Fix a pair P and abbreviate X = X_P. Construct a bipartite graph T_P as follows:

- one class of nodes consists of the face-components of X;
- the other consists of the face-components of Xᶜ;
- every component γ of ∂X gives one edge joining the components on its two sides.

Here face-components are taken using edge adjacency. In a cubic map this also captures topological connectivity: around a vertex, any two incident face sectors of the same side class share the third incident edge, so no additional connection is created only at a point.

**Contact-tree theorem** [PROVED]. T_P is a tree.

*Proof.* The components of ∂X = F_P are pairwise disjoint Jordan curves. Start with the sphere and insert these curves one at a time. A new curve lies inside one existing complementary region and splits that region into two. In the adjacency graph of the complementary regions, this operation replaces one node by two nodes joined by one new edge. Starting from one node and no edges, every insertion therefore preserves connectedness and acyclicity. The complementary regions are exactly the components of X and Xᶜ, with the side class alternating across every curve. Hence the resulting adjacency graph is T_P. ∎

Two useful consequences are immediate.

**Cycle-count corollary** [PROVED].

number of P-Kempe cycles = c(X) + c(Xᶜ) − 1.  (9.8)

**Tree-edge criterion** [PROVED]. Two P-colored edges lie on the same P-Kempe cycle exactly when they have the same X-side component and the same Xᶜ-side component; equivalently, they represent the same edge of T_P.

The rooted tree contains more information than the boundary word and its cycle partition: after rooting at the component containing the ocean, it also records the nesting depth and the unique region path between visible cycles. It does not, by itself, describe how the other two contact trees will change after a foreign switch.

### 9.12 CONDITION-1 as a coastal region identity

Consider a coastal move with working pair P = {U,V}. Let E_a, E_b be the split edges and let L_a, L_b be the land faces immediately behind them. Since the ocean has face color 0 and the split edges have colors in P, both land faces belong to X_P, while the ocean belongs to X_Pᶜ.

Let Q_oc be the component of X_Pᶜ containing the ocean.

**Coastal region criterion** [PROVED].

CONDITION-1 ⇔ L_a and L_b lie in the same component of X_P.  (9.9)

*Proof.* Both split edges have the same X_Pᶜ-side component, namely Q_oc. By the tree-edge criterion, they lie on the same P-Kempe cycle exactly when their X_P-side components also coincide. ∎

Consequently every P-Kempe cycle visible on the coastline is represented by an edge of T_P incident to the single root node Q_oc. The working-pair part of a coastal window therefore sees a star of the contact tree. Under CONDITION-2, the two land components are distinct nodes P_a, P_b, and their tree path is

P_a — Q_oc — P_b.  (9.10)

Repairing the move means altering the coloring so that the two marked land faces become members of one working-side component, while retaining the common ocean-side component.

### 9.13 Region gluing under one foreign switch

Fix a working pair P, put X = X_P, and let Δ be a foreign Kempe cycle. Choose the disk D bounded by Δ that does not contain the ocean. After switching Δ, equation (9.7) gives X′ = X △ D.

For an edge e ∈ ∂X ∩ Δ, call the contact

- **aligned** when the X-side face of e lies inside D;
- **opposed** when the X-side face lies outside D.

**Region-gluing theorem** [PROVED].

1. The components of X′ are obtained from the components of X\D and D\X by gluing across the opposed contacts of ∂X ∩ Δ.
2. The components of X′ᶜ are obtained from the components of Xᶜ\D and X∩D by gluing across the aligned contacts.

*Proof.* As face sets,

X′ = (X\D) ∪ (D\X),  X′ᶜ = (Xᶜ\D) ∪ (X∩D).  (9.11)

A face in the first piece of the first union can be adjacent to a face in the second piece only across an edge that changes both X-membership and D-membership. Such an edge lies in ∂X ∩ Δ, and its X-side is outside D, exactly the opposed case. Conversely every opposed contact gives such an adjacency. The complementary statement is identical with aligned and opposed exchanged. ∎

This gives an exact one-switch test in the coastal setting.

**Single-switch coastal repair theorem** [PROVED]. Assume CONDITION-2 for working pair P, and let Δ be a foreign Kempe cycle containing neither split edge. Choose D not containing the ocean. Then L_a, L_b lie outside D, and switching Δ produces CONDITION-1 exactly when the components of X\D containing L_a, L_b become joined, through components of D\X, by a chain of opposed contacts.

*Proof.* If a land face behind a split edge lay inside D while the ocean lay outside, that split edge would belong to the boundary Δ, contrary to the hypothesis. Thus both marked land faces remain in X′ and retain the ocean component on their complementary side. The coastal region criterion reduces the desired conclusion to their membership in one component of X′; the region-gluing theorem gives exactly the stated opposed-chain condition. ∎

Two necessary conditions follow immediately:

1. D\X must be nonempty; otherwise the flip cannot bridge two components of X\D.
2. Opposed contacts must reach the territories of both marked land components. A disk whose relevant contacts are all aligned cannot merge them.

This theorem decides one already selected switch. It does not prove that a suitable disk exists, nor does it describe the supply of admissible disks after later switches, because those disks are boundaries in the other two evolving contact trees.

### 9.14 One-switch terminal compression

Fix a foreign switch on a specified Kempe cycle K, and fix a set S of m marked edges. For incidence bookkeeping, regard each marked edge as carrying a formal terminal in its interior; no graph operation is performed. A marked edge can enter or leave an affected foreign 2-factor during the switch, so one must record both membership and cycle incidence.

Let

S_before, π_before; S_after, π_after

be the marked-edge subsets lying in the affected 2-factor before and after the switch, together with their partitions into Kempe-cycle components. Equivalently, label each path represented by an edge of the transition pairing, and each alternating edge of K, with the marked terminals it contains; the two closures in (9.2) then give the two partial partitions.

**One-switch terminal-compression theorem** [PROVED]. For a specified switch and m specified marked edges, the complete information relevant to their old and new membership and same-cycle relations is

(S_before, π_before; S_after, π_after).

Its size is bounded in terms of m alone, independently of the length of the switched cycle and of the size of the map.

*Proof.* Membership is given by two subsets of S. On each subset, cycle incidence is an equivalence relation and therefore a set partition. The marked, path-labeled transition pairing determines the old and new partial partitions by the two closures Q(K)∪P_A and Q(K)∪P_B. Conversely, this data answers every membership and same-cycle question concerning the marked edges before and after the switch. A crude upper bound is 4^m·B_m², where B_m is the m-th Bell number. ∎

For a window of at most eight edges, m ≤ 8, so a single chosen switch always has a finite terminal certificate. This proves a bounded-output statement. It does not give a closed transition system, because the next switch may use another Kempe cycle whose certificate is not determined by the current one.

### 9.15 An old terminal partition is not transition-closed

The failure of closure already occurs if one records only the old connectivity partition of marked terminals in the affected 2-factor, even with the two-page planarity constraint.

Let K be a hexagon with vertices 0,1,2,3,4,5 in cyclic order. Let its two alternating matchings be

P₀ = {01, 23, 45},  P₁ = {12, 34, 50}.

Consider the two transition pairings

Q₁ = {01, 24, 35},  Q₂ = {01, 25, 34}.

Both are two-page pairings: in Q₁ the crossing chords 24 and 35 are drawn on opposite sides of K; Q₂ is noncrossing on one side. Realize them by drawing K as an alternating A,B hexagon and adding the three C-colored matching edges. The resulting graphs are planar Tait-colored cubic multigraphs.

Mark the C-edges as a,b,c: in Q₁ they are respectively 01,24,35, and in Q₂ they are 01,25,34. All three marked edges belong to the affected {A,C} factor both before and after the {A,B} switch. Closing with P₀ gives the same marked partition in both graphs:

{a} | {b,c}.

Closing instead with P₁ gives

Q₁ ∪ P₁: {a,b,c},
Q₂ ∪ P₁: {a,b} | {c}.

These identities follow by following the alternating matching edges around each closure. Thus the example uses actual marked edges and the same old membership data as well as the same old partition. Its realizations contain parallel edges; it proves non-closure for the general planar cubic-multigraph transition system, without asserting realizability in every restricted family of pilot intermediates.

**Old-terminal non-closure theorem** [PROVED]. The old connectivity partition of marked terminals in one affected Kempe system, even together with the two-page planarity condition, does not determine the new terminal partition after a foreign switch.

Thus a finite certificate for one specified transition must contain genuinely new information about that transition. This example does not rule out a richer transition-closed descriptor coupling all three rooted contact trees; it shows only that one old terminal partition is insufficient.

### 9.16 Window incidence and the visible separator

The incidence graph of §3.4 gives an exact window-level picture of an R2-core. Let Λ_φ[W] denote the four-edge submultigraph consisting only of the incidence edges contributed by e₀, e₁, e₂, e₃. This notation is essential: the same four cycle-nodes may have additional incidences through edges outside the window.

**Window incidence theorem** [PROVED]. In an R2-core, Λ_φ[W] has exactly the four edges

X—H (via e₀),  H—Y (via e₂),  H—K (via e₁),  Y—K (via e₃).

Thus its underlying simple graph is the triangle H—Y—K—H with the pendant edge X—H.

*Proof.* Each window edge belongs to exactly two of the three 2-factors and therefore contributes exactly one incidence: e₀ has color A and lies on X ∈ F_AC and H ∈ F_AB; e₁ has color B and lies on H ∈ F_AB and K ∈ F_BC; e₂ has color A and lies on H and Y; e₃ has color C and lies on Y ∈ F_AC and K ∈ F_BC. ∎

**Incidence-distance degeneracy theorem** [PROVED]. In every R2-core, the distance in Λ_φ between the two marked working-pair cycles X and Y is exactly 2, realized by the visible path X—H—Y.

*Proof.* Distance 1 is impossible because X and Y belong to the same pair {A,C}, while incidence edges join nodes belonging to different pairs. The window incidence theorem exhibits a path of length 2. ∎

The degeneracy theorem has a decisive negative consequence: the unrestricted incidence distance between the marked cycles is **constant on all R2-cores** and therefore cannot serve as a descent potential. The displayed geodesic passes through H, one of the four visible switches whose exact one-switch test is already given in the table of §9.4.

The 2-connectivity theorem turns the existence of a remote chain into an exact separator question.

**Remote-chain separator criterion** [PROVED]. An R2-core admits a remote incidence chain from X to Y if and only if X and Y lie in the same component of Λ_φ − {H,K}. If no remote chain exists, then {H,K} is an inclusion-minimal X–Y vertex separator.

*Proof.* By the local-switch exhaustion theorem, X, Y, H, K are exactly the cycle-nodes that meet the window. Hence every internal node of an X–Y path in Λ_φ − {H,K} is remote, and every remote incidence chain avoids H and K. This proves the equivalence. If no such path exists, {H,K} separates X from Y. Neither H nor K alone can do so, because Λ_φ has no cut vertex. ∎

### 9.17 Primal separators, inherited cut bounds, and hybrid repair

Let G be the **current** Tait-colored map of an R2-core, with the cycles X,Y,H,K of §9.4, and put

B* = E(H) ∩ E(K).

Every edge of B* has color B. The set B* is a matching. Write δ_G(S) for the edge cut between S and its complement; a **bond** is an inclusion-minimal nonempty edge cut, equivalently a cut whose two induced sides are connected.

#### Component correspondence

**Component theorem** [PROVED]. The connected components of Λ_φ − {H,K} correspond canonically to the connected components of G − B*. Each surviving Kempe cycle belongs entirely to its corresponding primal component.

*Proof.* Every cycle except H,K avoids B*: {A,C}-cycles contain no B-edge, and another cycle of either remaining pair is edge-disjoint from H or K. Incidences between surviving cycles therefore stay inside a component of G − B*.

Conversely, associate to each primal vertex v its {A,C}-cycle AC(v), which always survives. Across an A- or C-edge the two associated nodes coincide. Across a B-edge e outside B*, at least one of its {A,B}- or {B,C}-cycles survives; the vertex triangles at its endpoints then give a surviving path between their AC-nodes. Finally, any surviving {A,B}- or {B,C}-cycle is adjacent to AC(v) at each of its vertices. These observations prove both directions of the component correspondence. ∎

**Separator–bond theorem** [PROVED]. The pair {H,K} separates X from Y in Λ_φ if and only if G has a bond F ⊆ B* separating V(X) from V(Y).

*Proof.* The component theorem gives a cut contained in B* exactly when the two marked cycles are separated. Choose an inclusion-minimal edge set within that cut still separating the marked cycles. Since each marked cycle is connected, any extra component of the deletion would allow a deleted edge to be restored without joining the marked sides. Hence the minimal set is a bond. The converse follows directly from the component correspondence. ∎

#### Geometry and size of the bond

**Bond-geometry theorem** [PROVED]. Let F = δ_G(S) ⊆ B* be such a bond, with X in S and Y in its complement S̄. Then:

1. F is a monochromatic matching of even size, and both sides contain cycles.
2. e₁ belongs to F; the other three window edges do not.
3. **Exactly two** coastline edges belong to F.
4. H and K are the only Kempe cycles meeting both sides, and each crosses every edge of F. In particular |E(H)| ≥ 2|F| and |E(K)| ≥ 2|F|, with no asserted equality of their lengths.
5. The dual Jordan curve γ crossing F runs entirely through the face-region Q_oc of colors {0,B}, and separates the two marked {A,C}-side land components.

*Proof.* Item 1 is the color-sum and monochromatic-cut lemma. The common vertex of e₀,e₁ belongs to X, whereas the common vertex of e₁,e₂ belongs to Y; hence e₁ crosses F. The other window edges lie in the {A,C} factor and cannot cross a B-cut.

A plane bond is dual to a simple cycle. Thus at every face the dual cycle uses either zero or two incident dual edges: every facial boundary contains either zero or two edges of F. It uses the ocean, since e₁ ∈ F, proving item 3. This argument also covers a dual cycle of length two in a multigraph.

Every cycle except H,K avoids F by the component theorem. Since F lies in both H and K, each crosses it exactly |F| times. Alternation gives the two independent length bounds.

Consecutive faces on γ differ by B. One is the ocean of color 0, so all have color 0 or B and belong to its connected complementary region Q_oc. The marked land faces have colors A and C and lie on opposite sides of γ; their connected {A,C}-regions cannot cross that curve. ∎

If the current graph itself is cyclically 5-edge-connected, the even size in item 1 is at least 6. The relevant excavation intermediates need not have that connectivity. The correct inherited bound is the following.

**Cut-lifting lemma** [PROVED]. Suppose r coastal additions reconstruct M ∈ C from a current map N. Every cyclic cut F of N lifts to a cyclic cut of M of size at most |F|+r. Consequently |F| ≥ 5−r.

*Proof.* Retain the vertex bipartition defining F. At each subdivision assign new vertices along an old crossing edge so that exactly one of its pieces crosses the partition; assign all subdivisions of an internal edge to its original side. Each inserted arc contributes at most one additional crossing. A cycle contained in either original side remains a subdivided cycle in that side. Induction over the r additions produces the stated cyclic cut of M, whose size must be at least 5. ∎

For a monochromatic cut before T₂ or T₁, r is respectively 2 or 1. Parity therefore gives **|F| ≥ 4** in both cases. The same lower bound holds in a one-pentagon deletion intermediate. With only one restoring move left, a four-edge cut must be crossed by the restoring arc; otherwise a cyclic four-cut would survive in M. For the separating bond above, its arc joins a subdivision point of e₀ in S to one of e₃ in S̄, so this crossing occurs.

#### One-sided renaming and its exact test

**One-sided renaming lemma** [PROVED]. For a B-monochromatic cut δ_G(S), exchanging A,C on all edges internal to S and fixing the other edges is a Kempe sequence.

*Proof.* Every {A,C}-cycle avoids the cut and lies wholly on one side. Switching all such cycles in S performs exactly the prescribed recoloring. ∎

For the separating bond, define

Θ = (H ∩ G[S]) ∪ F ∪ (K ∩ G[S̄]).

**Hybrid-cycle theorem** [PROVED]. The graph Θ is a disjoint union of cycles. After one-sided renaming on S, it is exactly the union of the new {B,C}-cycles that cross F. The move then satisfies CONDITION-1 if and only if e₀ and e₃ lie in the same component of Θ. One-sided renaming on S̄ has exactly the same success criterion.

*Proof.* Removing F cuts H and K into arcs inside the two sides. Each endpoint of a cut edge belongs to exactly one relevant arc, so Θ is 2-regular. After renaming on S, the new {B,C} factor equals the old {A,B} factor inside S and the old {B,C} factor outside. Every crossing component therefore alternates arcs of H in S and K in S̄.

The word becomes CBAC. The {A,C} factor is unchanged and still separates the endpoints, so only {B,C} can satisfy CONDITION-1. Both marked edges belong to Θ, which proves the test. Renaming on S̄ gives ABCA and tests the new {A,B} factor; its crossing components form the same Θ. ∎

**Visible-switch rigidity** [PROVED]. Switching H or K cannot give an immediate repair in the presence of this bond.

*Proof.* Switching H replaces F_BC by F_BC △ E(H), removing every edge of F from that factor. Its new cycles cannot join the two marked sides. The one-switch table shows that this is the only candidate working pair. Switching K has the analogous effect on F_AB. ∎

This is a statement about immediate repair. Either switch may still be used within a longer sequence.

#### Cut pairings and the coastal constraint

Number the 2m cut edges in cyclic order along γ. The H- and K-arcs define noncrossing perfect matchings μ_H,μ_K on S and μ_H′,μ_K′ on S̄. These are multigraph unions retaining one edge from each matching even when their endpoint pairs coincide. Since H and K are single cycles, μ_H ∪ μ_H′ and μ_K ∪ μ_K′ are connected alternating cycles. The hybrid uses μ_H ∪ μ_K′; a coincident pair of matching edges forms an alternating 2-cycle.

The two coastal cut edges, e₁ and a unique second edge g, are consecutive in this cyclic order: the dual cycle passes directly between them through the ocean. Thus an abstract collection of four noncrossing matchings is not by itself a realization of a coastal core; it must also preserve these marked positions and the local window. Both one-sided renamings test the same hybrid. No second, independent marked test is inferred for μ_K ∪ μ_H′.

**Coastal-pair repair lemma** [PROVED]. If μ_K′ pairs e₁ with g, one-sided renaming repairs the move. In particular every such separator core with |F| = 2 is repairable.

*Proof.* Work in the disk on the S̄ side of γ, extending each cut arc to its crossing point on γ. The curve γ cuts the disk ocean along a single proper arc τ, so the portion of the ocean on the S̄ side is connected. The K-arc joining the two ocean contacts and τ bound a Jordan disk containing that portion: the ocean is incident to τ on this side and its interior cannot cross K. All other K-arcs have endpoints on the complementary boundary interval and, being disjoint, cannot enter this Jordan disk. Since e₃ is a coastal K-edge on the S̄ portion of the coast, it must lie on the K-arc joining e₁ to g. The H-arc containing e₀ also ends at e₁, so Θ joins the two marked edges. For |F| = 2 there is only one arc on each side. ∎

For |F| = 4, there are just two noncrossing perfect matchings on four cyclically ordered terminals: pairing adjacent terminals in one of the two possible ways. Because the H- and K-closures are connected, μ_H′ differs from μ_H and μ_K′ differs from μ_K. Hence a disconnected hybrid requires μ_H = μ_K′. If the marked edges are also separated, the common matching cannot pair the two ocean contacts, by the coastal-pair repair lemma. This restricts a failed four-edge separator; it does not exclude it or rule out a later repair.

The unresolved separator problem is therefore to exploit the **realizable, coastally marked** hybrid structure, with the cut bounds appropriate to the current graph. Excluding a bond only in graphs of class C would not settle the intermediate-map problem.

**Remote rigidity of a separator core** [PROVED]. Starting from a separator core, a remote switch cannot produce an immediate {A,C} repair. A remote {B,C}-switch always gives another R2-core. A remote switch in either other pair either gives another core or separates the {B,C}-cycles through e₁,e₃, allowing one further cleaning switch.

*Proof.* Only H and K meet F, and each is visible. A remote switch therefore fixes every B-colored cut edge, so the {A,C} factor still cannot cross F or join the marked sides. A {B,C}-switch also preserves its own factor and the marked incidence. For the other pairs, the only remaining test is whether that incidence survives; if it breaks, cleaning e₁ gives ACAC. ∎

Consequently, the complementary-cycle realization of the R2 toggle consists entirely of remote core-to-core switches in every separator core, without requiring hostility. A repair may require changing the marked {B,C} incidence or using a visible switch before a later remote one.

### 9.18 Remote switches, core persistence, and hostile classes

By the local-switch exhaustion theorem, every Kempe switch other than the four visible ones is **remote**: it is disjoint from the window and leaves the word ABAC unchanged, together with the forced working pair {A,C}. The surgery law makes the effect of a remote switch on the two core conditions completely explicit, and the result is a rigid trichotomy.

**Remote trichotomy theorem** [PROVED]. Perform one remote switch on an R2-core. Exactly one of the following holds for the new coloring.

1. **Repaired.** The endpoint edges e₀, e₃ lie on one {A,C}-cycle; CONDITION-1 holds for the forced pair.
2. **Repairable in one more switch.** The endpoint edges still lie on distinct {A,C}-cycles, but the {B,C}-cycles through e₁ and e₃ are now distinct; switching the {B,C}-cycle through e₁ then produces the alternating word ACAC, and CONDITION-1 holds.
3. **Core again.** The new coloring is again an R2-core.

*Proof.* A remote switch fixes the colors of the four window edges, so the word remains ABAC. By the foreign-switch surgery theorem, the effect on the two core conditions depends only on the pair of the switched cycle. A remote {A,C}-switch leaves the {A,C} 2-factor unchanged, so the endpoint condition persists and only the marked {B,C}-condition can change. A remote {B,C}-switch leaves the {B,C} 2-factor unchanged, so the marked condition persists and only the endpoint condition can change. A remote {A,B}-switch can change both.

If the endpoint edges land on one {A,C}-cycle, this is case 1. If they remain separated and the marked condition breaks, the {B,C}-cycle through e₁ avoids e₃, and, containing no A-edges, it also avoids e₀ and e₂; switching it turns e₁ into C and yields the word ACAC. The four window edges are then consecutive edges of the {A,C} 2-factor, hence lie on one cycle, and CONDITION-1 holds for the forced pair {A,C}: case 2. If both conditions survive, the three defining properties of the core hold again: case 3. ∎

Cases 1 and 2 end the endgame move. Case 3 says that the only way a remote switch can fail is by reproducing the full core, never a weaker intermediate state.

**Remote realization of the R2 toggle** [PROVED]. If an R2-core is hostile, then its paired core under the canonical R2 toggle is reachable by a sequence in which every step is a remote core-to-core switch.

*Proof.* By the complementary-cycle form, the toggle is the product of the switches on all {B,C}-cycles other than the marked cycle K. Each of those cycles is remote, and the {B,C} 2-factor is unchanged throughout. Starting from a hostile core, an intermediate remote switch cannot enter case 1 or case 2 of the remote trichotomy, because either case would make the Kempe class repairable. Hence every intermediate state is again an R2-core. ∎

Combined with the normal-form theorem, the remote trichotomy gives a complete normal-form description of every hostile class, though it does not exclude such a class.

**Hostile-class theorem** [PROVED]. Call a Tait coloring of the intermediate map **hostile** for the fixed gap-2 move when the move is not repairable from it. Suppose some R2-core is hostile, and let 𝒦 be its Kempe equivalence class. Then:

1. every coloring in 𝒦 is hostile;
2. the alternating word never occurs in 𝒦, in any renaming;
3. every coloring in 𝒦 whose canonical window word is ABAC is an R2-core;
4. every coloring in 𝒦 whose canonical window word is ABCA has both candidate pairs in CONDITION-2, and switching the {A,C}-cycle through its two consecutive {A,C}-window edges sends it to an R2-core inside 𝒦.

*Proof.* Full Kempe switches are involutions, so reachability by finite switch sequences is a symmetric, transitive relation and repairability is constant on the class: (1). Every gap-2 coloring has canonical word ABAB, ABAC or ABCA. The alternating word satisfies CONDITION-1 in its forced pair, contradicting hostility: (2). A hostile ABAC-coloring has its forced pair in CONDITION-2, and the {B,C}-cycles through e₁ and e₃ must coincide, since otherwise one further switch inside 𝒦 reaches a coloring in CONDITION-1; hence it is an R2-core: (3). A hostile ABCA-coloring has both candidate pairs in CONDITION-2, since CONDITION-1 would repair the move directly. As in the normal-form theorem, the {A,C}-cycle through its two consecutive {A,C}-window edges avoids the first endpoint; switching it produces a coloring in 𝒦 with word ABAC, which by (3) is an R2-core: (4). ∎

The canonical R2 toggle restricts to an involution on the core set of 𝒦. It therefore cannot produce a descent by repeated application alone.

Five consequences narrow the target.

- **Normal form of a hostile class.** In a hostile class, every canonically written coloring is an R2-core or an ABCA state one switch from an R2-core. The uniform R2 escape problem is exactly the exclusion of such hostile classes.
- **A sufficient route through the alternating word.** Reaching the alternating word from some coloring of the class repairs the move, so it suffices to prove that the alternating gap-2 word is reachable in every Kempe class of every gap-2 state. A repair certificate may instead stop as soon as CONDITION-1 is obtained at ABAC or ABCA. Necessity of alternating-word reachability within a fixed class is not asserted.
- **No connected working factor.** If some coloring of a hostile class had a connected 2-factor in an admissible working pair, the two split edges would lie on its unique cycle and CONDITION-1 would hold. Hence in every ABAC-coloring of a hostile class the {A,C} 2-factor is disconnected, and in every ABCA-coloring both the {A,B} and the {A,C} 2-factors are disconnected.
- **No one-node incidence obstruction.** The graph Λ_φ is 2-vertex-connected, and the unrestricted distance between X and Y is always 2. Thus hostility cannot be explained by disconnected loop systems or by a single separating cycle-node. If a remote chain is absent, the remote-chain separator criterion forces the specific visible pair {H,K} to be an X–Y separator.
- **A primal dichotomy.** By the separator–bond theorem, every hostile R2-core falls into exactly one of two cases: either the map carries a B-monochromatic bond inside E(H) ∩ E(K) separating the two marked working cycles, with the current-map bounds and exact hybrid test of §9.17, or a remote incidence chain exists and the failure has to be produced by the dynamics along that chain.

A uniform sufficient target is the following.

**Uniform R2 escape target [OPEN].** For every gap-2 move arising in the specified excavation intermediates, prove that every Kempe class containing an R2-core also contains a coloring satisfying CONDITION-1. The weaker target R₅ in §10 needs only one successful class for one eligible deletion.

In region language, hostility implies that no reachable canonical state admits an endpoint-avoiding foreign disk satisfying the repair test of §9.13. The absence of such a single-disk bridge has not been proved sufficient for hostility. The region approach is developed as a target in §12.4. The incidence approach splits into the bond case of §9.17 and the remote-chain problem of §12.3.

| (5,5,7) | 11 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 10 | 8 | 6 | T₃ solved; gap 2 plus gap 4 |
| (5,5,8) | 12 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 12 | 10 | 8 | T₃ solved; gap 2 plus gap 5 |
| (5,5,9) | 13 | 1 | 2 | 6 | 14 | 12 | 10 | T₃ solved; gap 2 plus gap 6 |
| (5,6,6) | 11 | 1 | 3 | 3 | 10 | 4 | 4 | T₃ solved; two gap-3 moves |
| (5,6,7) | 12 | 1 | 3 | 4 | 12 | 5 | 3 | T₃ solved; gap 3 plus gap 4 |

The first count is the full pilot-multiplicity count from §5.5. The second retains only role assignments with a pentagon in the f₃ position, so that the Pentagon Opening Theorem applies to T₃. The third also imposes the exact ordering displayed in the gap columns: among the two remaining faces, the smaller is f₂. Thus the (5,6,7) row has five pentagon-first pilots, but only three with f₃=5, f₂=6, and f₁=7. The two later moves are sequentially coupled: switches and half-switch choices made for T₂ alter the starting coloring for T₁.

**Weighted pilot theorem** [PROVED]. Every M ∈ C has at least 120 pilots satisfying the displayed order, hence at least 120 pentagon-first pilots and at least 120 ordered pilots in total.

*Proof.* Let d(v) be the displayed-order count at a positive vertex. For (5,5,5) and (5,5,6), the table gives d(v) = 10c(v). For each other type, d(v) ≥ 3 while c(v) ≤ 9/35 < 3/10. Thus d(v) ≥ 10c(v) at every positive vertex. Since the nonpositive vertices contribute at most zero,

Σ_{c(v)>0} d(v) ≥ 10 Σ_{c(v)>0} c(v) ≥ 120.

Each counted pilot has a specified vertex and ordered faces, so summing creates no duplicate pilot. The pentagon-first and unrestricted sets contain the displayed-order set. ∎

The bound is attained by the dodecahedral map, with twenty (5,5,5) corners and six ordered pilots per corner. The count supplies choice, not a guarantee that one coloring extends. Gap 2 has the normal form of §9.4; gap 3 has only the local transition rules of §9.5; larger gaps have the conditional word-normalization procedure of §9.3.

## 10. The single-pentagon reduction

### 10.1 Five smaller maps

Let M ∈ C and fix any pentagonal face f. Such a face exists because Euler's law gives

F[5] = 12 + Σ_{n≥7}(n−6)F[n] ≥ 12.

For each boundary edge t of f, let f_ω(t) be the other incident face. Declare f_ω(t) to be the ocean, delete t, and suppress its two endpoints. Denote this excavated map by G_t, retaining the marked window for reinserting f. The two faces share only t, so f is clean. The excavation results in a smaller bridgeless, loopless cubic plane map. Its inverse creates a pentagon, hence has gap 2.

The five maps G_t are regarded as labelled reductions with their distinct marked moves, even if some are abstractly isomorphic. Their membership in C is not assumed.

### 10.2 A canonical bijection

Keep the three color names fixed. Write Tait(N) for the set of Tait colorings of N and Alt(G_t) for the subset in which the four-edge reinsertion window alternates two colors.

**Pentagon-bijection theorem** [PROVED]. For every fixed pentagon f there is a canonical bijection

$$
\operatorname{Tait}(M)\;\longleftrightarrow\;
\bigsqcup_{t\in E(\partial f)}\operatorname{Alt}(G_t).
$$

*Proof.* Take a Tait coloring ψ of M. Each color appears at most twice on the pentagonal boundary, because equal-colored edges cannot be consecutive on that cycle. Two colors cannot color an odd cycle. Thus the three multiplicities are exactly 2,2,1.

Let t = pq be the unique boundary edge of color W, and let U,V be the other colors. Along the remaining four-edge boundary path from p to q the colors are U,V,U,V, after choosing the names in that order. The third, non-boundary edges at p and q then have colors V and U respectively.

Delete t, interchange U,V on the entire four-edge boundary path, and suppress p and q. At each endpoint the two edges to be fused now have the same color. Every internal path vertex still sees all three colors. The resulting coloring φ is a Tait coloring of G_t, and its four-edge window is V,U,V,U. This defines the map to the disjoint union.

Conversely, take φ ∈ Alt(G_t). Subdivide the two marked endpoint edges and half-switch along the coastal arc between the subdivision points. Give the new arc t the third color W. The contiguous alternating window makes this a valid half-switch extension. On the resulting pentagon, t is the unique W-edge and the other four boundary edges alternate U,V. The deletion construction therefore returns exactly (t,φ). Applying these operations in the other order also returns ψ. ∎

There is no factor of two: the inverse has specified the coastal half-switch arc. The other half-switch choice is represented by a different old coloring, obtained by a full switch on the common working cycle.

**Counting identity** [PROVED].

$$
|\operatorname{Tait}(M)|
=\sum_{t\in E(\partial f)}|\operatorname{Alt}(G_t)|.
$$

This is a structural bijective identity, requiring no enumeration of colorings.

**Existential equivalence** [PROVED]. For a fixed pentagon f, the following are equivalent:

1. M is Tait-colorable.
2. Some G_t has a coloring with an alternating reinsertion window.
3. Some G_t has a coloring from which its fixed reinsertion move is repairable by Kempe switches and a half-switch.

*Proof.* The theorem gives 1 ⇔ 2. An alternating window satisfies CONDITION-1, giving 2 ⇒ 3. A successful reinsertion colors M, giving 3 ⇒ 1. The passage from 3 back to 2 may select a different edge of the same pentagon; it makes no assertion about alternating-word reachability in one fixed Kempe class. ∎

### 10.3 The essential open target

**Existential pentagon-reinsertion statement R₅ [OPEN within this program].** Let M ∈ C and let f be a pentagon such that all five maps G_t are Tait-colorable. Then at least one of these maps has a coloring from which the marked reinsertion is repairable.

By the equivalence above, its conclusion can be written

$$
\sum_{t\in E(\partial f)}|\operatorname{Alt}(G_t)|>0.
$$

Minimality provides |Tait(G_t)| > 0 for every t; it does not provide |Alt(G_t)| > 0 for any t. The counting identity transfers the question exactly but does not establish this positivity. A structural proof of that implication is the missing step.

The conclusion is existential over both the deleted edge and its coloring. It requires neither repairability of every coloring of one intermediate nor escape from every Kempe class of every gap-2 move. The uniform R2 target of §9.18 is sufficient and stronger than needed on this family of intermediates.

### 10.4 A common dual graph for the five excavations

Let T = M* and let v be the degree-five vertex corresponding to f. Write its neighbours as a₀,a₁,a₂,a₃,a₄ in cyclic order; all subscripts in §§10.4–10.9 are taken modulo 5. Their cycle is induced: a chord between nonconsecutive neighbours, together with v, would form a separating triangle, contrary to 5-connectivity.

Delete v and put Q = T − v. Regard the resulting pentagonal face as the outer face, so Q is drawn in a closed disk with boundary a₀a₁a₂a₃a₄. For each i define

$$
Q_i=Q+\{a_i a_{i+2},\,a_i a_{i+3}\}.
$$

Thus Q_i fills the pentagonal hole by the fan centred at a_i. The two added diagonals share their centre and do not cross.

**Common-dual lemma** [PROVED]. If t_i is the primal edge dual to va_i, then Q_i is the dual of the excavation G_{t_i}. In particular, the five dual coloring spaces can all be regarded as subsets of the coloring space of Q.

*Proof.* Deleting t_i in the primal contracts va_i in the dual. Identify the contracted vertex with a_i. Its two edges to each of a_{i−1},a_{i+1} become parallel; suppressing the two degree-two primal vertices removes the corresponding duplicate dual edges. The other two edges formerly incident with v become a_i a_{i+2} and a_i a_{i+3}. All other edges remain those of Q. The induced boundary ensures that these are new diagonals. ∎

Write G_i = G_{t_i}. Fix the four vertex-color labels V₄ throughout, without fixing the color of any vertex, and define

$$
\Omega=\operatorname{Col}_4(Q),\qquad
\Omega_i=\operatorname{Col}_4(Q_i)\subseteq\Omega.
$$

Every Ω_i consists of colorings of the same vertex set. Its additional requirements are just the two fan-edge inequalities. This common normalization is essential when taking intersections. By Tait's correspondence,

$$
|\Omega_i|=4|\operatorname{Tait}(G_i)|,\qquad
|\operatorname{Col}_4(T)|=4|\operatorname{Tait}(M)|.
$$

The factor four counts the possible global additive translations of the face colors. No quotient by global color permutations has yet been taken.

### 10.5 Boundary sectors and exact overlaps

A proper coloring of a five-cycle uses at least three colors, and each color occurs at most twice. Hence every element of Ω has one of two boundary multiplicity patterns:

| boundary pattern | terminology | extension to v |
|---|---|---|
| 2,2,1 | good | unique: use the missing fourth color |
| 2,1,1,1 | bad | impossible with the boundary colors fixed |

“Bad” refers only to this boundary assignment. It does not assert that no other coloring can extend.

Let E_i be the good colorings whose singleton boundary color occurs at a_i. Let B_i be the bad colorings whose repeated boundary color occurs at a_i and a_{i+2}. These five pairs are exactly the nonadjacent pairs on the five-cycle. Put

$$
g_i=|E_i|,\quad b_i=|B_i|,\qquad
g=\sum_i g_i,\quad b=\sum_i b_i.
$$

These ten sectors partition Ω. Forgetting the color of v gives a bijection from Col₄(T) to the union of the good sectors, so

$$
g=|\operatorname{Col}_4(T)|=4|\operatorname{Tait}(M)|.
$$

**Fan-membership theorem** [PROVED]. A coloring belongs to Ω_i exactly when the color of a_i occurs only once on the boundary. Consequently,

$$
\Omega_i
=E_i\;\sqcup\;B_{i+1}\;\sqcup\;B_{i+2}\;\sqcup\;B_{i+4}.
$$

Every good coloring belongs to exactly one fan space; every bad coloring belongs to exactly three. In particular, Ω = ⋃ᵢ Ω_i.

*Proof.* Properness on Q already separates the color of a_i from the colors of its two boundary neighbours. The two added diagonals require separation from the other two boundary vertices as well. Thus a_i must have a singleton color. A good boundary has one singleton position, and a bad boundary has three. In B_j the forbidden fan centres are a_j and a_{j+2}, which gives the displayed decomposition. ∎

Under the face-to-edge correspondence, E_i is exactly the alternating-window subset for G_i, with its four translations retained:

$$
g_i=4|\operatorname{Alt}(G_i)|.
$$

Indeed, if the singleton color at a_i is γ, the other four boundary colors alternate α,β along the path avoiding a_i. Their edge differences with γ alternate the two values γ+α, γ+β. These are precisely the four colors in the marked primal window, including its fused endpoint edges. Conversely, an alternating window gives that boundary pattern.

**Overlap identity** [PROVED]. Adjacent fan spaces satisfy

$$
\Omega_i\cap\Omega_{i+1}=B_{i+2},
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{\;
g=\sum_i|\Omega_i|
   -3\sum_i|\Omega_i\cap\Omega_{i+1}|.
\;}
$$

*Proof.* A good coloring cannot belong to two different fans. For a bad coloring to belong to both Ω_i and Ω_{i+1}, its repeated pair must avoid a_i,a_{i+1}. Among the three remaining vertices, the only nonadjacent pair is a_{i+2},a_{i+4}. This is B_{i+2}. Summing the membership formula gives Σᵢ|Ω_i| = g+3b, while summing these five intersections gives b. Subtraction proves the identity. ∎

This identifies the overlap that has to be controlled. Nonemptiness of the five fan spaces alone does not make the right-hand side visibly positive.

### 10.6 Vertex Kempe exchanges: extension or transfer

The common graph Q allows a different kind of comparison. A **vertex Kempe change in Q** swaps two vertex colors on one connected component of the subgraph induced by those colors. It preserves proper coloring of Q.

These changes need not preserve the extra diagonals of a particular Q_i. They therefore compare the five fan spaces without being asserted to form an edge-Kempe sequence in any one G_i. If a good coloring is found, it colors T and then M; §10.2 supplies an alternating coloring of some excavation. This proves the existential conclusion required by R₅, but does not establish repairability of an arbitrary originally chosen coloring in its own edge-Kempe class.

For c ∈ B_i ∪ B_{i+2}, call c **linked at i** if a_{i+1} and a_{i+3} lie in the same component of the subgraph induced by their two colors. Otherwise call it **unlinked at i**.

**Exchange lemma** [PROVED]. There are:

1. a bijection between the colorings of B_i linked at i and the colorings of B_{i+2} linked at i, realized by one vertex Kempe change;
2. an injection from the unlinked colorings of B_i into E_{i+4}, realized by one vertex Kempe change;
3. an injection from the unlinked colorings of B_{i+2} into E_i, realized by one vertex Kempe change.

*Proof.* Rotate the indices so that i = 0. For a coloring in B₀, write its boundary as

$$
(\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\gamma,\delta),
$$

where α,β,γ,δ are distinct.

First suppose a₁ and a₃ are linked by a βγ-path. Choose a simple such path. Its only boundary vertices are a₁,a₃. The endpoints a₀,a₂ alternate with a₁,a₃ around the boundary disk. The Jordan separation theorem therefore forbids an αδ-path from a₂ to a₀: the two paths would have to meet, while their color pairs are disjoint. Since a₀ and a₄ are adjacent in the αδ-subgraph, the αδ-component through a₂ also misses a₄.

Swap α,δ on that component. Among the boundary vertices only a₂ changes, and the boundary becomes

$$
(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\gamma,\delta)\in B_2.
$$

The βγ-subgraph is unchanged, so the result is still linked at 0. The αδ-subgraph is unchanged as an uncolored induced subgraph, so switching its same component through a₂ reverses the operation. Starting with any linked coloring of B₂, the same separation argument excludes a₀,a₄ from that component and gives a coloring of B₀. This proves the bijection.

Now suppose a₁ and a₃ are unlinked. Swap β,γ on the component containing a₁. Starting from B₀, the new boundary is

$$
(\alpha,\gamma,\alpha,\gamma,\delta)\in E_4.
$$

Starting from B₂ with boundary (α,β,δ,γ,δ), it is

$$
(\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\gamma,\delta)\in E_0.
$$

Each operation is injective. From an output in its image, identify the color missing from the boundary and the common color at a₁,a₃. Swap these two colors on the component through a₁. The induced subgraph and its components have not changed, so this recovers the input. Rotation proves all three statements for arbitrary i. ∎

Thus a bad coloring either gives an extension after one test, or admits a controlled transfer to another bad sector. The transfer is reversible. Its existence does not force a later extension.

### 10.7 Quantitative sector comparison

**Sector-comparison theorem** [PROVED]. For every i,

$$
\boxed{\;-g_i\le b_i-b_{i+2}\le g_{i+4}.\;}
$$

In particular,

$$
\boxed{\;
g\ge \max_i b_i-\min_i b_i.
\;}
$$

*Proof.* Partition B_i and B_{i+2} into their linked and unlinked subsets at i. The linked subsets have the same size by the exchange lemma. Write u_i,u'_{i+2} for the sizes of the two unlinked subsets. The injections give

$$
0\le u_i\le g_{i+4},\qquad
0\le u'_{i+2}\le g_i,\qquad
b_i-b_{i+2}=u_i-u'_{i+2}.
$$

This proves the first inequality. The index graph joining i to i+2 is a five-cycle. Any two indices are at distance at most two in this graph. Along an oriented edge the upper bound is one g_j; along a shortest two-edge path the two upper bounds use distinct g_j. Adding them gives b_i−b_j ≤ Σ_k g_k for any i,j. Choose a maximum and a minimum to obtain the second inequality. ∎

Consequently, unequal bad-sector counts already force a good coloring and a repairable excavation. The theorem also gives a lower bound on the number of extensions. It is a comparison obtained from actual Kempe components and planar separation, without enumerating colorings.

### 10.8 Balanced bad classes and the arithmetic obstruction

A **vertex-Kempe class of Q** is an equivalence class generated by its vertex Kempe changes. Call such a class entirely bad if it contains no good coloring. This is distinct from the hostile edge-Kempe classes of §9.18.

**Balanced-class theorem** [PROVED]. If a nonempty vertex-Kempe class L of Q is entirely bad, then for some positive integer m_L,

$$
|L\cap B_i|=24m_L\quad\hbox{for every }i,
$$

and

$$
|L|=120m_L,\qquad |L\cap\Omega_i|=72m_L.
$$

*Proof.* An unlinked coloring in either sector of the exchange lemma would give a good coloring by one Kempe change, contradicting the definition of L. Hence, within L, the linked-subset bijections cover the whole sectors. They show

$$
|L\cap B_i|=|L\cap B_{i+2}|.
$$

Addition by two runs through all five indices. Since L is nonempty, all five sector sizes are equal and positive.

Every global permutation of the four vertex colors preserves L: a transposition is realized by switching all components of the corresponding two-color subgraph, and transpositions generate S₄. It also preserves every sector B_i. The S₄-action on each bad sector is free, because all four labels occur on its boundary. Hence each sector L ∩ B_i has size divisible by 24. Write the common size as 24m_L. The formulas for L and its fan subsets now follow from the five-sector partition and the three-sector fan-membership formula. ∎

This theorem concerns sets of Q-colorings. Their projections to Tait(G_i) are not asserted to be single edge-Kempe classes.

**Necessary counting condition for failure of R₅** [PROVED]. Suppose all five excavations are Tait-colorable and no coloring of M exists. With fixed edge-color names, put

$$
N_i=|\operatorname{Tait}(G_i)|.
$$

Then a positive integer m exists such that

$$
\boxed{\;N_0=N_1=N_2=N_3=N_4=18m.\;}
$$

*Proof.* Here Ω is nonempty and g = 0. All its vertex-Kempe classes are entirely bad. Sum their positive integers m_L to obtain m. Every bad sector has size 24m, so every fan space has size 72m. Dividing by the four additive translations gives N_i = 18m. ∎

Equivalently, if colorings differing by a global permutation of A,B,C are identified, the five resulting counts must all be equal to 3m. The division by six is valid because every Tait coloring uses all three colors.

**Sufficient certificates for R₅** [PROVED]. Assume all five excavations are Tait-colorable. Either of the following is sufficient for the conclusion of R₅:

- two excavations have different numbers of Tait colorings;
- one excavation has a number of Tait colorings not divisible by 18.

*Proof.* Either condition contradicts the necessary condition for g = 0. Thus g > 0, and §§10.2 and 10.5 give an alternating coloring of an excavation and hence a repairable reinsertion. ∎

These are sufficient certificates, not a claim that every map in C satisfies one of them. Equal counts divisible by 18 can occur without violating these tests; the tests alone decide nothing in that case.

### 10.9 The exact remaining balanced case

The common-space formulation gives an equivalent target:

**Existential degree-five extension target [OPEN within this program].** For the graph Q = T − v arising above,

$$
\Omega\ne\varnothing\quad\Longrightarrow\quad g>0.
$$

This target and R₅ are equivalent. The displayed implication immediately supplies the conclusion of R₅. Conversely, assume R₅ and take a nonempty Ω. If g were zero, the balanced-class theorem would put a coloring in every fan space. All five excavations would then be colorable, so R₅ would imply a coloring of M and hence g > 0, a contradiction.

Thus, in a hypothetical failure, even one coloring of Q generates enough bad colorings to meet all five fan spaces. Their simultaneous nonemptiness supplies no further contradiction in that case.

The unresolved step is to exclude a nonempty Ω made entirely of balanced bad classes, or to construct a good coloring outside those classes. The proved relations admit the numerical pattern

$$
g_i=0,\qquad b_i=24m,\qquad |\Omega_i|=72m
\quad(m\ge1).
$$

This is a formal solution of the established count constraints, not an example of a planar counterexample. Further structure is required to rule out its realization. In particular, cycling through the five bad sectors gives no strictly decreasing quantity and no termination argument toward a good boundary.

Boundary-coloring counts are also studied by Dvořák and Lidický in *Coloring count cones of planar graphs*. Their Lemma 7 relates exclusion of an exceptional degree-five counting ray to the Four Color Theorem, and their Conjecture 9 proposes a stronger inequality. That work places this positivity question in an existing framework; neither its finite cone enumeration nor an unproved inequality is used in the arguments above. See the [authors' paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.04066).

## 11. Conditional assembly and the optional pilot route

### 11.1 Assembly from R₅

**Single-pentagon conditional theorem** [CONDITIONAL]. R₅ implies that every bridgeless cubic plane map is Tait-colorable, and hence implies the Four Color Theorem.

*Proof.* Choose a non-Tait-colorable bridgeless cubic plane map M with the fewest vertices. The classical reduction puts M in C. Euler's law supplies a pentagon f. Each G_t is smaller and bridgeless, so each is Tait-colorable by minimality. Statement R₅ supplies a repairable coloring for one of these five marked moves. Reinserting f gives a Tait coloring of M, a contradiction. ∎

Conversely, the known Four Color Theorem implies R₅ through the pentagon-bijection theorem. Thus R₅ is an equivalent target under the classical reductions; its reformulation is not a proof of the missing implication.

### 11.2 The three-face statement

A **pilot** is a positive vertex, an assignment of its incident faces to f₁,f₂,f₃, and a compatible closing face. It determines M⁻_A and the three final additions. A **colored pilot** is a pair (A,φ) with φ ∈ Tait(M⁻_A).

**Endgame statement R_existential [OPEN within this program].** For every M ∈ C with at least one colored pilot, there exist a pilot A, a starting coloring φ of M⁻_A, and compatible choices of switches and half-switches that perform T₃,T₂,T₁ in sequence.

Here “in sequence” includes the quantifier over the actual intermediate colorings: the chosen repair of T₂ must produce a coloring from which T₁ is repairable. The existence of unrelated successful input colorings for the two moves does not suffice.

**Pilot conditional theorem** [CONDITIONAL]. R_existential also implies the Four Color Theorem.

*Proof.* In a minimal counterexample every pilot intermediate is smaller and bridgeless, and hence colorable. A successful colored pilot reconstructs a coloring of the complete map, contradicting minimality. ∎

A uniform variant, under the same hypothesis that all pilot intermediates are colorable, asks for one pilot that works from every starting coloring. Its conclusion is stronger under that common hypothesis. No uniformity is needed in either conditional assembly.

The pilot route and the single-pentagon route are alternative sufficient programs. Gap-3 and larger-gap work remains relevant to the former; it is not required if R₅ is proved directly.

## 12. Structural directions for the remaining implication

### 12.1 Exclude the balanced boundary case

Sections 10.4–10.8 construct the comparison among the five deletions. They identify a common dual graph Q, the five fan spaces, their exact overlaps, and Kempe bijections or injections between boundary sectors. These relations force equal bad-sector counts when no good coloring exists. The remaining question is what additional planar structure makes an entirely bad coloring space impossible.

One possible route is a further inequality forcing g > 0 whenever Ω is nonempty. Another is a choice of vertex Kempe changes that reaches a good sector, with an argument that prevents indefinite return among the bad sectors. Neither follows from the present reversible transfers. Unequal fan counts and the divisibility certificate settle some cases, but are not proved to occur for every pentagon.

The existential target still permits a successful coloring in any class and any fan. A hostile edge-Kempe class for one excavation, or an entirely bad vertex-Kempe class of Q, would not itself disprove R₅: another class could supply a good coloring. A proof using Q must also respect the distinction between its vertex Kempe changes and the full edge-cycle switches of one fixed excavation.

### 12.2 Lift the word procedure through actual cycles

Section 9.3 provides a terminating abstract normalization for every proper window. Its obstruction to graph realization is an explicit return of a selected cycle to another window component. A useful lifting theorem would resolve such returns while retaining a well-founded measure, or show that at least one of the five pentagon deletions avoids an inescapable pattern of returns.

The word potential alone cannot do this: it is defined for the prescribed sparse transitions, and a blocked transition is not available in the graph. Likewise, no scalar can strictly decrease along *every* core-to-core Kempe switch, because such switches are reversible. A descent proof must select an available direction or identify a state with no decreasing move as already repairable.

### 12.3 Exploit separators or remote chains

For an R2-core, the component theorem gives a precise dichotomy: either X and Y are joined in Λ_φ − {H,K}, or a B-monochromatic bond inside E(H) ∩ E(K) separates them.

In the bond case, the exact test is the marked hybrid system of §9.17. The dual cycle meets the ocean exactly twice; a pairing joining those two contacts on the relevant K-side repairs the move. A two-edge separator therefore repairs, while a four-edge separator has the restricted matching form described there. In the actual one- or two-move intermediates, the inherited lower bound is four. Any further exclusion or repair must use those intermediates and their marked geometry.

In the remote-chain case, the existence of an incidence path does not produce a sequence of successful switches. A remote switch can reroute several cycles on that path. The remote trichotomy gives the correct alternatives: repair, repair in one more switch, or a full core again. A proposed descent should assign a well-founded quantity to the latter transitions and prove the availability of at least one lowering switch.

The ordinary incidence distance between X and Y is always two in an R2-core and cannot serve this purpose. A remote distance might contribute to a richer potential, but no monotonicity is established here.

### 12.4 Control regions without losing future switches

The transition pairing and region-gluing theorem determine the result of a *specified* foreign switch. The rooted contact trees retain nesting and component information, but switching a cycle of one pair changes the other two trees. A before/after terminal certificate gives bounded output for that chosen transition; it does not encode the supply of subsequent cycles.

Possible approaches are a transition-closed descriptor coupling all three systems, or a directional topological argument that finds a successful disk without describing every future switch. Neither is supplied by the old-terminal partition example or by the connectedness of the incidence graph alone.

### 12.5 Optional joint piloting

The weighted count supplies at least 120 pilots in the displayed order. The choice of arc color, half-switch arc, and preliminary Kempe switches for T₂ changes the input for T₁, so a joint two-move argument might be more useful than separate uniform repair claims.

Counting pilots by itself does not show that one works. Different pilots generally have different intermediate maps and coloring spaces. Any averaging or incompatibility argument about their obstructions requires an explicit relation between these spaces. Likewise, the gap-3 transition rules need a termination argument before the two named patterns can be treated as exhaustive targets.

## 13. Logical status

The structural results establish legal excavation to theta, controlled three-face piloting, the seven positive corner types, and the weighted lower bound of 120 pilots in the displayed order. The single-pentagon bijection gives an exact five-way decomposition of the colorings of a map with a chosen pentagon. The common-dual lemma realizes its five excavation coloring spaces on one vertex set and identifies their overlaps.

The coloring results establish the half-switch criterion, the complete gap-2 normal form with its two-switch bound, exact foreign-switch surgery, contact trees, region gluing, and the incidence-to-primal component correspondence. They also give the hybrid repair criterion, the exact two-contact coastal constraint, and the inherited cut bounds for current maps.

For the common dual graph Q, the exchange lemma gives actual vertex Kempe bijections between linked bad sectors and injections from unlinked sectors into good ones. It yields quantitative comparison bounds and proves that every entirely bad vertex-Kempe class has five equal boundary sectors. Consequently, a global failure of reinsertion requires five equal positive Tait-coloring counts divisible by 18. These are necessary conditions for failure and sufficient certificates when violated.

The abstract cleaning–shifting procedure terminates when its sparse operations are available. The gap-3 rules are exact local transitions. These two statements do not establish that every required graph-level transition exists or that the gap-3 routes terminate.

The essential missing implication is R₅, equivalently the assertion that nonempty Ω contains a good coloring. The unresolved case has only balanced bad classes. The proved identities and inequalities do not exclude that formal counting pattern or establish a successful sequence from it. Uniform R2 escape, a coupled contact-tree argument, and the optional three-face endgame remain other possible sufficient routes.

## 14. Dependency structure

The two sufficient routes have different remaining hypotheses:

| route | established reduction | hypothesis needed for reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| one pentagon | five smaller colorable maps, common dual space, exchange and counting criteria | exclude the balanced case to prove R₅ |
| three-face pilot | at least 120 displayed-order pilots with colorable intermediates | R_existential supplies compatible sequential repairs |

In either row, the specified reconstruction would color the alleged minimal counterexample and give a contradiction.

The coastal pilot construction gives a second route to the same contradiction if its sequential endgame statement is proved. The exact Kempe and region calculi support either route. Their role is to formulate and analyze the remaining existence problem; they do not settle it.

## 15. Glossary

| term | meaning |
|---|---|
| Ocean | distinguished disk face during construction. |
| Coastline | boundary cycle of the ocean. |
| Move | subdivision of two coastal edges plus insertion of one arc. |
| Promotion | increase by one in the size of a face behind a split edge. |
| Sealing | loss of the last coastal edge of a face. |
| Excavation | reverse of a coastal move. |
| Clean face | shore face with one connected coastal contact, hence one coastal edge. |
| Pilot | a positive corner, the ordered roles f₁,f₂,f₃, and a compatible closing face; it determines M⁻ and the last three additions. |
| Excavation continuation | any legal completion from M⁻ to theta; it certifies a coastal construction but does not change the endgame state. |
| Working pair | the two colors different from the new arc color; admissible when it contains both split-edge colors. |
| CONDITION-1 | the split edges lie on the same Kempe cycle of the selected pair. |
| CONDITION-2 | they lie on distinct cycles. |
| Repairable | reachable, by full Kempe switches, from a coloring satisfying CONDITION-1. |
| Boundary word | color sequence on the two split edges and the gap edges between them. |
| P-run | maximal block of consecutive window edges colored with the working pair; s denotes their number, and s = 1 implies CONDITION-1. |
| Cleaning switch | the switch that removes an easy foreign letter and merges two runs. |
| Shifting switch | the switch that exchanges a foreign letter with a neighbouring window edge. |
| Easy / hard foreign letter | a window letter of the third color whose two neighbours have equal / different colors. |
| Easy two-run R2 signature | the additional cycle incidence blocking the cleaning of a unique easy foreign letter between two runs. |
| Boundary Kempe signature | for each color pair, the cycle-equivalence relation induced on the window edges of that pair. |
| Transition pairing | the pairing of the vertices of a switched Kempe cycle induced by paths of an affected foreign 2-factor after its alternating matching on that cycle is removed. |
| Domain wall | one of the three two-color 2-factors, viewed as the boundary of a binary face-color field. |
| Working-side region X_P | the union of faces whose colors are the two colors of working pair P, with the ocean in the complementary side. |
| Contact tree | the bipartite tree of components of X_P and X_Pᶜ, with one edge for each P-Kempe cycle. |
| Incidence graph Λ_φ | one pair-labelled node per Kempe cycle of each of the three 2-factors; one incidence edge per edge of G, joining the two cycles of different pairs that contain it. Its underlying simple graph is 2-vertex-connected whenever G is connected. |
| Window incidence subgraph Λ_φ[W] | the incidence edges contributed by the current boundary window only; it need not be the full subgraph induced by the visible cycle-nodes. |
| Remote incidence chain | a path in Λ_φ between the two marked working cycles whose interior nodes are all remote cycles. In an R2-core it exists exactly when H and K do not separate X from Y. |
| Visible two-cycle separator | the pair {H,K}; it separates X from Y exactly when the map carries a monochromatic bond inside E(H) ∩ E(K). |
| Monochromatic bond | a minimal edge cut of one color; a matching of even size with cycles on both sides. Its size is at least 6 in class C, and at least 4 in the one- or two-move intermediates considered here. |
| One-sided renaming | the exchange of A and C on one side of a B-monochromatic cut; a Kempe sequence, being the product of the switches on the {A,C}-cycles of that side. |
| Hybrid cycle | a component of the system obtained by concatenating the arcs of H inside the cut with the arcs of K outside it; it decides the one-sided renaming test. |
| Foreign disk | the ocean-free disk bounded by a Kempe cycle of a pair different from the current working pair. |
| Aligned/opposed contact | a common edge of a working boundary and a foreign switch cycle, classified by whether the working-side face lies inside/outside the foreign disk. |
| Region bridge | an opposed-contact chain created by a disk flip that joins the two marked land components. |
| Terminal certificate | the before/after pair of cycle partitions induced by one specified switch on a fixed marked set. |
| R2-core | the canonical unresolved gap-2 state ABAC with distinct endpoint {A,C}-cycles and one marked {B,C}-cycle through the internal B and final C. |
| R3a-core, R3b-core | two specified blocked gap-3 patterns, with words ABABC and ABACB; their exhaustiveness as normal forms is not proved. |
| R2 toggle | switching the marked {B,C}-cycle and canonically exchanging B,C; equivalently, switching every other {B,C}-cycle. If it fails, it moves to an involutively paired R2-core, which may be the same core. |
| Remote switch | a Kempe switch disjoint from the current boundary window; it preserves the word but can alter foreign cycle incidences. |
| Remote trichotomy | the exact classification of a remote switch on an R2-core: repair, repair-in-one-more-switch, or core again. |
| Hostile class | a Kempe equivalence class from which a fixed move is never repairable. For gap 2, canonical colorings are R2-cores or their one-switch ABCA neighbours. |
| Positive corner | one of the seven face-size triples in §6. |
| Ring | boundary of the union of the three faces at a positive corner. |
| Endpoint cleaning | recoloring a foreign-colored window endpoint using the reference-pair color absent from its sole window neighbour. |
| Alt(G_t) | Tait colorings of a labelled pentagon-deletion intermediate with an alternating four-edge reinsertion window. |
| R₅ | the existential reinsertion statement for the five deletions of a fixed pentagon. |
| Common dual graph Q | T − v, where T = M* and v corresponds to the chosen pentagon. |
| Fan space Ω_i | all four-label vertex colorings of Q satisfying the two added diagonals centred at a_i; it has four times as many elements as Tait(G_i). |
| Good sector E_i | Q-colorings using three boundary colors, with the singleton color at a_i; each extends uniquely to v. |
| Bad sector B_i | Q-colorings using four boundary colors, with the repeated color at a_i,a_{i+2}; the fixed boundary does not extend to v. |
| Vertex Kempe change in Q | swapping two vertex colors on one induced bichromatic connected component; it may leave a particular fan space. |
| Entirely bad vertex-Kempe class | a class of Q-colorings containing no good boundary coloring; its five bad sectors have equal sizes divisible by 24. |
| Cut lifting | extending a vertex bipartition through subdivision and arc insertion, adding at most one crossing per move. |
